Buying paint is one of those jobs where guessing costs you twice: buy too little and you make a second trip (and risk a slightly different tint batch), buy too much and you have expensive cans in the garage for a decade. The good news is that the maths is simple. You need four numbers — the area you are painting, the number of coats, the coverage of your paint, and a small allowance for waste — and you can work them out with a tape measure in ten minutes.

This guide walks through each step, explains why coverage varies so much between rooms, and finishes with a full worked example. If you would rather skip the arithmetic, the [paint calculator](/calculators/paint-calculator) does it for you.

## The paint formula in one line

> **Gallons = paintable area × coats ÷ coverage per gallon × (1 + waste %)**

Everything else in this article is about getting those four inputs right.

## Step 1: Measure the wall area

Walls are rectangles, so wall area is just perimeter times height.

1. Measure the length of each wall at roughly waist height, in feet.
2. Add them together to get the room's perimeter. For a rectangular room, that is 2 × (length + width).
3. Measure the wall height from floor to ceiling. Standard ceilings in many US homes are 8 feet, but 9 and 10 feet are common in newer houses — always measure.
4. Multiply perimeter by height.

![Diagram of a 12 by 14 foot room unfolded into four walls, each 8 feet tall, with a door and two windows marked for subtraction](/images/blog/how-much-paint-do-i-need/wall-area-diagram.svg)

For a room with a sloped ceiling, a stairwell or a gable wall, split the wall into rectangles and triangles. A triangle is half of base × height. The [wall area calculator](/calculators/wall-area-calculator) handles multiple walls of different sizes if your room is not a simple box. For more on odd shapes, see our guide on [how to calculate square footage](/blog/how-to-calculate-square-footage).

## Step 2: Subtract doors and windows

You do not paint glass or the door slab (unless you are painting the door separately), so take those areas off:

| Opening | Typical size | Area to subtract |
|---|---|---|
| Standard interior door | 3 ft × 6 ft 8 in | about 20 sq ft |
| Average window | 3 ft × 5 ft | about 15 sq ft |
| Sliding patio door | 6 ft × 6 ft 8 in | about 40 sq ft |
| Double closet doors | 5 ft × 6 ft 8 in | about 33 sq ft |

Measure anything unusual, such as a large picture window or a wide archway. Do not subtract small items like outlets or switches; they are part of your rounding.

## Step 3: Decide how many coats

The coat count has the biggest effect on your total, because it multiplies everything.

- **One coat:** Same colour (or very close), sound existing paint, quality product. Common for a refresh.
- **Two coats:** The standard for any colour change and for most walls. Even "paint and primer in one" products generally perform best with two coats.
- **Primer plus two coats:** New drywall, patched or stained walls, glossy surfaces, or big changes such as dark red to off-white.
- **Three or more coats:** Some deep, saturated colours (bright reds and yellows especially) hide poorly. A grey-tinted primer usually solves this more cheaply than extra topcoats.

If you are unsure, the [number of coats calculator](/calculators/number-of-coats-calculator) walks through the surface and colour change, and the [primer calculator](/calculators/primer-calculator) sizes the primer coat separately — primer usually covers less area per gallon than finish paint because porous surfaces soak it up.

## Step 4: Use a realistic coverage figure

Every can lists a coverage range, usually something like "250–400 sq ft per gallon". That range exists because the surface matters as much as the paint. The figures below are typical planning numbers; always check the label of the product you are buying.

| Surface | Typical planning coverage per gallon |
|---|---|
| Smooth, previously painted drywall | 350–400 sq ft |
| Light texture (orange peel, knockdown) | 300–350 sq ft |
| Heavy texture or popcorn ceiling | 200–300 sq ft |
| New, unprimed drywall (primer coat) | 200–300 sq ft |
| Exterior wood siding | 250–350 sq ft |
| Stucco, brick or block | 100–250 sq ft |

When in doubt, use **350 sq ft per gallon** for interior walls. It is conservative enough that you rarely come up short, but not so low that you over-buy by a full gallon.

## Step 5: Add a waste allowance

Some paint never reaches the wall: it soaks into rollers, stays in the tray, and gets used on cutting-in and touch-ups. A **10% allowance** covers this for most rooms. Go to 15% for heavily textured surfaces or if you are using a sprayer, which loses more material to overspray. You can read more about how allowances differ across materials in our [material waste factor guide](/blog/material-waste-factor-guide).

## Worked example: a 12 × 14 ft bedroom

Here is a typical bedroom from start to finish:

- Room: 12 ft × 14 ft, 8 ft ceilings
- One standard door, two average windows
- Changing from beige to a mid-tone blue: two coats
- Paint coverage on the label: 350–400 sq ft per gallon (we use 350)

**Wall area:** perimeter = 2 × (12 + 14) = 52 ft. Wall area = 52 × 8 = **416 sq ft**.

**Subtract openings:** 1 door (20) + 2 windows (2 × 15 = 30) = 50 sq ft. Paintable area = 416 − 50 = **366 sq ft**.

**Coats:** 366 × 2 = **732 sq ft** of coverage needed.

**Gallons:** 732 ÷ 350 = **2.09 gallons**.

**Waste:** 2.09 × 1.10 = **2.3 gallons**.

**What to buy:** 2.3 gallons rounds to **3 gallons**. Alternatively, buy 2 gallons and 2 quarts — but check prices, because quarts are much more expensive per ounce and 3 gallons is often only slightly more. The leftover becomes your touch-up supply.

### The ceiling

The ceiling is just length × width: 12 × 14 = 168 sq ft. Two coats of flat ceiling white at 350 sq ft per gallon is 336 ÷ 350 = 0.96 gallons, so **1 gallon** is tight; with 10% waste it is about 1.06 gallons. If you are refreshing a white ceiling with the same white, one coat (about half a gallon) is often enough. The [ceiling paint calculator](/calculators/ceiling-paint-calculator) handles this separately.

### The trim

Trim is small in area but usually painted in a harder-wearing semi-gloss. For a single room, baseboards, one door and its casing, and two window casings typically fit within **one quart** for two coats. For a whole floor of trim or several doors, buy a gallon. Our [paint sheen guide](/blog/paint-sheen-guide) explains why walls and trim usually get different sheens.

## Whole-house estimates

For a quick sense check before you measure every room, it helps to know that wall area is usually much larger than floor area. With 8-foot ceilings, a 10 × 10 ft room has 320 sq ft of wall on 100 sq ft of floor (3.2 times), and a 12 × 14 ft room has 416 sq ft of wall on 168 sq ft of floor (about 2.5 times). Bigger rooms have proportionally less wall — a 20 × 20 ft room is only 1.6 times. That is only a ballpark — measure room by room for the real order — but it explains why a "small" repaint often needs more paint than people expect.

A practical approach for multiple rooms:

1. List each room with its perimeter, height, openings and coat count.
2. Group rooms by colour and sheen, since that is how you buy paint.
3. Total the gallons for each colour and round up per colour, not per room.

## Gallons, quarts and 5-gallon buckets

Paint is sold in a few standard sizes, and the right mix can save real money:

| Size | Volume | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Sample | about 8 oz | Testing colours on the wall |
| Quart | 0.25 gallon | Trim, a single door, accent wall touch-ups |
| Gallon | 1 gallon | Most rooms |
| 5-gallon pail | 5 gallons | Whole floors, repeated colours, sprayers |

Prices vary widely by brand, region and sale, but a 5-gallon pail is often priced below the cost of five single gallons, so if your total for one colour is four gallons or more, compare the pail price. When you do buy several gallons of the same colour, pour them together into a larger bucket before you start — painters call this "boxing" — so any slight tint variation between cans is blended out.

## Common mistakes that throw estimates off

- **Measuring the floor instead of the walls.** Floor area tells you about flooring and ceilings, not walls.
- **Forgetting the second coat.** Doubling the area is the single most common correction.
- **Using the top of the coverage range.** "Up to 400 sq ft" is a best-case number on a smooth, sealed surface.
- **Skipping primer on new drywall.** Bare paper and joint compound absorb paint unevenly; finish paint over bare drywall often looks blotchy and needs an extra coat anyway.
- **Not rounding per colour.** Three rooms that each need 1.3 gallons of the same colour need about 4 gallons total, not 6.

## Quick reference

- Wall area = perimeter × height
- Subtract about 20 sq ft per door and 15 sq ft per window
- Two coats for most jobs; primer for new or patched surfaces
- Plan on 350 sq ft per gallon for smooth interior walls
- Add 10% for waste, then round up to the can sizes you can buy
