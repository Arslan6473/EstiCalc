Bathrooms are small, but they are dense. In a few dozen square feet you have water supply, drains and vents, electrical with ground-fault protection, an exhaust fan, waterproofing, tile and finish carpentry. That density is why bathroom remodels cost more per square foot than almost any other room, and why the price can swing so widely between two bathrooms that look similar.

This guide explains the major cost drivers, builds a line-by-line budget for a typical 5 × 8 foot bathroom, and shows where the surprises hide. Plug your own numbers into the [Bathroom Remodel Cost Calculator](/calculators/bathroom-remodel-cost-calculator) for a starting estimate.

## The four biggest cost drivers

### 1. Whether plumbing moves

Keeping the toilet, vanity and tub or shower in their current locations is the single most effective way to control cost. Moving a toilet drain or converting a tub to a walk-in shower with a new drain location involves new piping, venting, sometimes cutting floor joists, and inspections.

### 2. Shower and tub choices

A replacement acrylic tub with a prefabricated surround is very different in cost from a custom tiled walk-in shower with a curbless entry, linear drain, niche, bench and glass door. Tiled showers require a properly built waterproofing system, which is skilled labor.

### 3. Tile area and complexity

Floor-only tile is modest. Full-height wall tile, small mosaics, patterned layouts and natural stone multiply both material and labor. The [Bathroom Tile Estimator](/calculators/bathroom-tile-estimator) and [Shower Tile Calculator](/calculators/shower-tile-calculator) help size the tile order.

### 4. Labor rates in your area

Plumbers, electricians and tile setters charge very different rates from one metro area to another. A bathroom remodel may involve four or five trades, so local rates compound.

## Scope levels

| Scope | Typical work | Rough cost picture* |
|---|---|---|
| Cosmetic | Paint, new vanity top or faucet, mirror, lighting, accessories, maybe re-grout | Lowest; often DIY-friendly |
| Standard remodel | New tub or shower surround, vanity, toilet, floor tile, fan, same layout | Mid range |
| Full gut / upgrade | Down to studs, tiled shower, new layout, heated floor, custom vanity | Highest; can exceed a mid-range kitchen |

*Costs vary widely by region and date. Treat any national figure as a sanity check, not a quote.

## Worked example: budgeting a 5 × 8 bathroom

Consider a standard 5 × 8 foot hall bathroom: tub along the 5-foot back wall, toilet and vanity along one long wall. The plan is a standard remodel in the same layout: new tub, tiled surround, tile floor, new vanity, toilet, fan and lighting.

![Plan view of a 5 by 8 foot bathroom showing a 5 foot tub at the back wall, toilet and 30 inch vanity on the side wall, door opposite, with tile areas shaded](/images/blog/bathroom-remodel-cost-guide/diagram-1.svg)

### Material quantities

- **Floor tile:** 5 × 8 = 40 sq ft. Subtract the tub footprint (5 × 2.5 = 12.5 sq ft) = 27.5 sq ft. Add 10% waste = about **30 sq ft**.
- **Tub surround tile:** back wall 5 ft wide, two end walls 2.5 ft wide, all tiled to about 6 ft above the tub rim (72 inches; adjust to your design). Area = (5 + 2.5 + 2.5) × 6 = **60 sq ft**. Add 10–15% = about **66–69 sq ft**. Subtract nothing for the valve; it's a cut.
- **Cement board or foam board** for the surround: about 60 sq ft. At 15 sq ft per 3 × 5 sheet that is 4 sheets, so plan 4–5 to allow for cuts.
- **Grout and thinset** for roughly 100 sq ft of tile total. See [how to estimate tile and grout](/blog/how-to-estimate-tile-and-grout).
- **Paint** for the remaining walls and ceiling: usually one gallon of moisture-resistant bathroom paint plus primer. See [how much paint do I need](/blog/how-much-paint-do-i-need).

### Budget structure

Rather than guess dollar amounts for your region, structure the budget with these lines and fill each one from quotes:

| Line item | Notes |
|---|---|
| Demolition and disposal | Tub removal, tile, drywall; dumpster or haul fee |
| Plumbing rough-in and finish | New valve, drain, supply lines, set toilet and faucet |
| Electrical | GFCI protection, vanity light, fan circuit |
| Exhaust fan and ducting | Vented to the exterior, not the attic |
| Tub and drain | Tub, overflow/drain assembly |
| Wall board and waterproofing | Cement board or foam board, membrane |
| Tile materials | Floor and surround, trim, thinset, grout |
| Tile labor | Often the largest single labor line |
| Vanity, top, faucet | Stock vs custom changes cost a lot |
| Toilet | Plus wax ring or seal, supply line |
| Drywall, paint, trim | Patching where walls were opened |
| Accessories, mirror, door | Towel bars, shower rod or glass door |
| Permits | Required in many areas for plumbing and electrical |
| **Contingency** | **15–20% of the total** |

## Hidden costs that show up after demolition

Bathrooms hide problems behind tile. Common discoveries include:

- **Rotted subfloor** around the toilet flange or tub from slow leaks.
- **Damaged joists** that were notched or cut for old drains.
- **Old galvanized or polybutylene supply lines** that should be replaced while walls are open.
- **No exhaust fan**, or a fan venting into the attic, which leads to moisture and mold problems.
- **Ungrounded wiring** or missing GFCI protection.
- **Walls out of plumb** that need shimming before a tub or tile goes in.
- **Mold** behind the old surround.

This is why 15–20% contingency is common advice for bathrooms. In an older home, err toward the higher end.

## Where to spend and where to save

**Worth spending on:**

- **Waterproofing.** A failed shower pan or surround means tearing everything out. Use a proven system and follow its instructions exactly.
- **The shower valve.** A pressure-balancing or thermostatic valve is inside the wall for decades; replacing it later means opening tile.
- **Ventilation.** A quiet, properly sized fan vented outdoors protects every other finish.
- **Good tile installation.** Labor quality shows in flat floors, consistent joints and clean cuts.

**Places to save:**

- **Keep the layout.** No fixture moves, no new drains.
- **Use standard sizes.** A standard 60-inch tub and stock vanity cost far less than custom sizes.
- **Choose ceramic or porcelain** over natural stone; porcelain is durable and low maintenance.
- **Limit full-height tile** to the wet areas and paint the rest.
- **DIY the finishing work:** painting, accessories, mirror and caulking.

## Plan the timeline

Even a standard bathroom remodel takes weeks, not days, because trades work in sequence: demolition, plumbing and electrical rough-in, inspections, board and waterproofing, tile, grout cure time, fixtures, paint and final inspection. If it's your only bathroom, plan where everyone will shower in the meantime. Ordering the tub, valve, vanity and tile before demolition avoids idle days.

## Safety and permits

Bathroom work routinely involves plumbing and electrical systems close to water. Circuits serving bathroom receptacles generally require GFCI protection, and work on wiring or new circuits should be done by a licensed electrician where your jurisdiction requires it. Shut off water at the fixture or main before disconnecting anything, and don't cut into floor joists to route drains without guidance from a qualified professional, since that can weaken the floor structure. Old tile, joint compound or flooring in homes built before the 1980s may contain asbestos, and older paint may contain lead; have suspect materials tested before demolition.

## Does it pay back?

An updated bathroom can help a home sell, but it's rare to recover the full cost at sale. Remodel for your own use first and think carefully before overbuilding for the neighborhood. ## Quick checklist

- Decide whether any fixtures move; keeping the layout saves the most.
- Measure floor and wall tile areas and add waste.
- Build a line-item budget and fill it from local quotes.
- Add a 15–20% contingency for hidden damage.
- Spend on waterproofing, valve and ventilation.
- Pull permits and use licensed trades for plumbing and electrical.
