Square footage is the number behind almost every home-improvement estimate: flooring, paint for ceilings, tile, carpet, underlayment, insulation, even how big an air conditioner should be. It is also the measurement people most often get slightly wrong — not because the maths is hard, but because real rooms have bump-outs, angled walls, bay windows and dimensions in feet *and* inches.

This guide covers the basic formula, how to handle inches, and how to break any odd-shaped room into simple pieces you can add up. If you just want the number, the [square footage calculator](/calculators/square-footage-calculator) accepts multiple sections at once.

## The basic formula

For a rectangle or square:

> **Square feet = length (ft) × width (ft)**

A bedroom that measures 12 ft by 11 ft is 12 × 11 = **132 sq ft**. That is the whole idea; everything else is about turning messy rooms into rectangles and a few other simple shapes.

## Tools and measuring tips

- A 25-ft tape measure works for most rooms. A laser measure is faster for long runs and when furniture is in the way.
- Measure wall to wall at floor level, not along the baseboard top, and ignore trim thickness.
- Measure each dimension twice — once along each opposite wall. Older houses are rarely perfectly square, and walls can differ by an inch or more. Use the **larger** figure when you are buying material.
- Sketch the room on paper first and write every measurement on the sketch. It is much easier to spot a missing piece on a drawing than in a list of numbers.

## Converting feet and inches

Tape measures give you feet and inches, but the formula needs decimal feet. Divide the inches by 12:

| Inches | Decimal feet |
|---|---|
| 1 in | 0.08 ft |
| 3 in | 0.25 ft |
| 4 in | 0.33 ft |
| 6 in | 0.5 ft |
| 8 in | 0.67 ft |
| 9 in | 0.75 ft |
| 10 in | 0.83 ft |

So 14 ft 8 in becomes 14.67 ft. A common mistake is writing 14 ft 8 in as "14.8" — that understates the length, because 8 inches is two-thirds of a foot, not eight-tenths. ## L-shaped and irregular rooms

The trick for any room made of straight walls is to **divide it into rectangles that do not overlap**, calculate each one, and add them.

![L-shaped room split into a 20 by 12 foot rectangle and a 10 by 8 foot rectangle, totalling 320 square feet, with the alternative subtract method shown](/images/blog/how-to-calculate-square-footage/l-shaped-room.svg)

There are two ways to do it, and they always agree:

1. **Add method:** Split the L into two rectangles. In the diagram, 20 × 12 = 240 sq ft plus 10 × 8 = 80 sq ft gives **320 sq ft**.
2. **Subtract method:** Measure the full bounding rectangle (20 × 20 = 400 sq ft) and subtract the missing corner (10 × 8 = 80 sq ft). 400 − 80 = **320 sq ft**.

Use whichever needs fewer measurements in your room. Doing both is a good way to check your work. The [L-shaped room area calculator](/calculators/l-shaped-room-area-calculator) does this split for you, and the [room area calculator](/calculators/room-area-calculator) handles rooms with several sections.

For T-shapes, U-shapes and rooms with multiple alcoves, the same rule applies — just more rectangles.

## Triangles, circles and other shapes

Not every piece is a rectangle. These are the formulas you will meet in real homes:

![Formulas for the area of a triangle, circle, half-circle and trapezoid, each with an example dimension and result in square feet](/images/blog/how-to-calculate-square-footage/shape-formulas.svg)

| Shape | Where you see it | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Rectangle | Most rooms | length × width |
| Right triangle | Angled corner, a wall cut at 45° | ½ × base × height |
| Trapezoid | Bay window floor, angled hallway | ½ × (side A + side B) × depth |
| Circle | Round patio, turret room | 3.1416 × radius² |
| Half-circle | Curved bay, half-round step | 3.1416 × radius² ÷ 2 |

Remember that the radius is **half** the diameter. A 10-ft-wide round patio has a 5-ft radius: 3.1416 × 5 × 5 = 78.5 sq ft. The [circle and triangle area calculator](/calculators/circle-triangle-area-calculator) is handy for these pieces.

### Rooms with a cut-off corner

A room with one wall running at 45° across a corner is a rectangle with a triangle missing. Measure the full rectangle as if the corner were square, then subtract the triangle (½ × the two short legs). For example, a 14 × 12 ft room with a corner cut 3 ft along each wall: 168 − (½ × 3 × 3) = 168 − 4.5 = **163.5 sq ft**.

### Truly irregular spaces

For curved garden-style rooms or anything without a clear geometry, use the **offset method**: run a straight baseline down the middle of the space, measure out to the edge at regular intervals (say every 2 ft), and treat each strip as a thin trapezoid. Add the strips together. It is the same idea surveyors use, and it is accurate enough for buying materials when you add a sensible waste allowance.

## Worked example: an open living and dining area

Here is a realistic open-plan space with a bay window:

- **Main living area:** 18 ft 6 in × 13 ft 3 in = 18.5 × 13.25 = **245.1 sq ft**
- **Dining nook** (opens off one side): 9 ft × 7 ft 6 in = 9 × 7.5 = **67.5 sq ft**
- **Bay window** (trapezoid): front width 8 ft, back width 5 ft, depth 2 ft → ½ × (8 + 5) × 2 = **13 sq ft**

**Total:** 245.1 + 67.5 + 13 = **325.6 sq ft**

Converted:

- Square yards: 325.6 ÷ 9 = **36.2 sq yd** (useful for carpet, which is often priced per square yard — see the [square feet to square yards calculator](/calculators/square-feet-to-square-yards-calculator))
- Square metres: 325.6 × 0.0929 = **30.2 m²**

That 325.6 is the *net* area. To buy flooring you would add a waste allowance — typically 5–10% for plank flooring laid straight — so you would shop for about 345–360 sq ft. ## What counts and what doesn't

What you include depends on why you are measuring:

| Purpose | Include | Usually leave out |
|---|---|---|
| Flooring | Closets, alcoves, bay floors that get the same material | Areas under fixed cabinets or a bathtub |
| Ceiling paint | Full ceiling, including closets if painted | Skylight openings |
| Carpet | Everything carpeted, plus stair treads and risers separately | Hard-floor transitions |
| Heating or cooling | The conditioned floor area of the room | Unconditioned porches |
| Real estate listing | Finished, heated living space | Garages, unfinished basements (rules vary) |

Real estate square footage is a different exercise from measuring for materials. Appraisers and builders often follow a published standard — ANSI Z765 is widely used for single-family homes in the US — that measures from the **exterior** walls and treats below-grade and unfinished space separately. That is why the listed square footage of a house is larger than the sum of its room measurements: it includes wall thickness, hallways and stairwells.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating inches as decimals.** 10 ft 6 in is 10.5 ft, not 10.6 ft.
- **Double-counting overlap.** When you split an L-shape, make sure the rectangles meet at a line and don't share a strip.
- **Using diameter instead of radius** for circles, which quadruples the answer.
- **Measuring once.** Out-of-square rooms can be several inches different from wall to wall.
- **Forgetting closets and doorways** that receive the same flooring.

## Quick reference

- Rectangle: length × width
- Triangle: ½ × base × height
- Trapezoid: ½ × (a + b) × depth
- Circle: 3.1416 × radius²
- Inches ÷ 12 = decimal feet
- Square feet ÷ 9 = square yards; × 0.0929 = square metres
