A tile job has three materials to estimate — tile, the adhesive under it (thinset mortar) and the grout between it — and each one is calculated differently. Tile is about area and waste. Grout is about the volume of the joints. Thinset is about the trowel you use. Get all three right and you avoid both mid-job store runs and boxes of leftover tile you cannot return.

This guide works through each one, then puts them together in a worked example. The [tile calculator](/calculators/tile-calculator) and [grout calculator](/calculators/grout-calculator) will check your numbers.

## Step 1: Measure the area

Measure the floor or wall in feet and calculate the area in square feet. For floors, break L-shaped rooms into rectangles and add them (our guide on [how to calculate square footage](/blog/how-to-calculate-square-footage) covers odd shapes). For walls, measure each wall section separately and subtract windows, doors and large fixtures. The [wall tile calculator](/calculators/wall-tile-calculator) handles walls, and our [backsplash tile guide](/blog/backsplash-tile-guide) covers the outlets, windows and cabinet edges you meet in kitchens.

Do not subtract small items like a toilet flange or floor drain — you still have to cut tile around them.

## Step 2: Choose a waste factor

Tile waste comes from cuts at walls, around obstacles, breakage and the occasional tile with a chip or glaze flaw. Layout pattern matters most:

| Layout | Typical waste allowance |
|---|---|
| Straight grid, simple room | 10% |
| Offset (brick) pattern | 10–15% |
| Diagonal (45°) | 15% |
| Herringbone or chevron | 15–20% |
| Many corners, angled walls, or very large tiles | Add 5% to the above |

Large-format tiles (any side 15 in or more) can need more waste than you expect, because each cut-off piece is bigger and often cannot be reused elsewhere. Natural stone also deserves extra for colour sorting. See the [material waste factor guide](/blog/material-waste-factor-guide) for how tile compares with other materials.

## Step 3: Convert to tiles and boxes

Divide the area including waste by the area of one tile, or simply divide by the box coverage:

| Tile size | Area per tile | Tiles per sq ft |
|---|---|---|
| 3 × 6 in | 0.125 sq ft | 8 |
| 6 × 24 in | 1 sq ft | 1 |
| 12 × 12 in | 1 sq ft | 1 |
| 12 × 24 in | 2 sq ft | 0.5 |
| 18 × 18 in | 2.25 sq ft | 0.44 |
| 24 × 24 in | 4 sq ft | 0.25 |

Nominal sizes are approximate, and the joint adds a little to each tile's footprint, but for buying purposes this is close enough. Round up to whole boxes using the box coverage printed on the carton.

## Step 4: Plan the layout before you buy

A few minutes with a sketch can save tile and improve the look:

- Find the centre of the room and dry-lay a row each way to see where cuts fall.
- Shift the layout so you do not end up with thin slivers (less than about half a tile) along a visible wall or in a doorway.
- For large tiles in an offset pattern, industry guidance generally recommends an offset of no more than one-third of the tile length to limit lippage caused by slight bowing — check the manufacturer's instructions.
- Decide where trim pieces (bullnose, metal edge profiles, Schluter-style trims) go, and count them in linear feet.

## Step 5: Estimate grout

Grout fills the joints, so the quantity depends on the **total length of joints** and the **size of each joint**:

- **Tile size:** Smaller tiles mean more joints per square foot. A mosaic sheet can use several times more grout than large tiles.
- **Joint width:** A 1/4 in joint holds twice as much grout as a 1/8 in joint.
- **Joint depth:** Usually the tile thickness.

![Diagram of one 12 by 24 inch tile with a 1/8 inch joint on two sides, showing that each tile contributes 36 inches of joint and that joint volume equals length times width times depth](/images/blog/how-to-estimate-tile-and-grout/grout-joint.svg)

The joint length per square foot is:

> **Joint inches per sq ft = 144 × (tile length + tile width) ÷ (tile length × tile width)**, with tile size in inches

Multiply that by the joint width and depth (in inches) to get cubic inches of grout per square foot, then multiply by your area. Bagged cement grout typically fills somewhere around 15 cubic inches per pound of powder, but this varies by product, which is why the grout bag's own coverage chart is the final word. Add 10–15% for mixing loss and joint cleanup.

As a rule of thumb, joint width follows tile type: about 1/16 to 1/8 in for rectified porcelain, 1/8 to 3/16 in for most floor tile, and 1/4 in or more for handmade or rustic tile. Use sanded grout for joints wider than about 1/8 in and unsanded for narrower joints, unless the product says otherwise. Epoxy and urethane grouts have their own coverage charts.

## Step 6: Estimate thinset

Thinset coverage depends almost entirely on the trowel notch, which in turn depends on tile size and flatness. The aim is full mortar contact — especially on floors and in showers — so larger tiles need bigger notches and often back-buttering.

| Tile size | Common trowel notch | Approx. coverage per 50 lb bag |
|---|---|---|
| Mosaics, up to 2 in | 3/16 in V-notch | 100+ sq ft |
| Up to 8 in | 1/4 × 1/4 in square | about 90–100 sq ft |
| 8–16 in | 1/4 × 3/8 in square | about 60–70 sq ft |
| 15 in and larger | 1/2 × 1/2 in square, plus back-butter | about 40–50 sq ft |

These are typical figures; read the bag's chart and the tile manufacturer's recommendations. Large-format tile usually calls for a mortar rated for large and heavy tile. The [thinset calculator](/calculators/thinset-calculator) covers notch sizes.

## Worked example: a 9 × 11 ft laundry room floor

- **Area:** 9 × 11 = **99 sq ft**
- **Tile:** 12 × 24 in porcelain, 3/8 in thick, laid in a one-third offset, 8 tiles (16 sq ft) per box
- **Waste:** offset pattern with large tiles: 12%
- **Total:** 99 × 1.12 = **110.9 sq ft**
- **Tiles:** 110.9 ÷ 2 = 55.4 → **56 tiles**
- **Boxes:** 110.9 ÷ 16 = 6.9 → **7 boxes** (56 tiles, 112 sq ft)

**Grout** (1/8 in joint, 3/8 in deep):

- Joint length per sq ft: 144 × (12 + 24) ÷ (12 × 24) = 144 × 36 ÷ 288 = **18 in**
- Volume per sq ft: 18 × 0.125 × 0.375 = **0.84 cu in**
- Total: 0.84 × 99 = **83 cu in**
- At about 15 cu in per lb: 83 ÷ 15 ≈ 5.5 lb, plus 15% ≈ **6.4 lb**

A single 10 lb bag covers it with some to spare. Check the bag's chart for 12 × 24 tile with a 1/8 in joint to confirm.

**Thinset:** 12 × 24 tiles call for a 1/2 × 1/2 in notch and back-buttering. At roughly 40–50 sq ft per 50 lb bag, 99 sq ft needs 2–2.5 bags, so buy **3 bags**. Leftover unopened bags can often be returned.

## Don't forget the extras

- **Backer board or membrane** for showers and many floors — sized by sheet, like drywall.
- **Spacers or a levelling system**, especially for large-format tile.
- **Trim pieces** for exposed edges, counted in linear feet.
- **Transition strips** at doorways.
- **Sealer** for natural stone and for cement grout if the manufacturer recommends it.
- **Caulk** for changes of plane (inside corners, where floor meets wall or tub), where grout should not be used.

## Quick reference

- Tile = area × (1 + waste %), rounded up to whole boxes
- Waste: 10% straight, 15% diagonal, 15–20% herringbone
- Grout depends on tile size, joint width and depth — small tiles and wide joints use the most
- Thinset depends on trowel notch; bigger tiles need bigger notches and back-buttering
- Buy everything from the same lot, and keep spares
