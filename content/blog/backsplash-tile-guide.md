A kitchen backsplash is one of the most satisfying tile projects: the area is small, the visual impact is big, and the materials bill is modest compared with a floor. It is also one of the easiest projects to under-buy for. A backsplash is broken up by outlets, windows, corners and cabinet ends, so it uses a lot more cut tiles per square foot than a floor does.

This guide walks through planning the layout, measuring each section, choosing a waste factor, and turning square footage into tiles, boxes, trim, thinset and grout. When you have your numbers, the [Backsplash Tile Calculator](/calculators/backsplash-tile-calculator) will do the arithmetic for you.

## Decide where the backsplash starts and stops

Before you measure, decide the boundaries. A few common choices:

- **Counter to upper cabinets.** The most common layout. The tile runs from the countertop to the underside of the wall cabinets, often around 18 inches tall, though your kitchen may differ.
- **Full height behind the range.** Many people take the tile all the way to the ceiling or to the hood behind the cooktop, creating a feature panel.
- **Full height on open walls.** Where there are no upper cabinets (open shelving, a window wall), the tile may run to the ceiling or stop at a consistent height.
- **Side ends.** Decide whether the tile stops at the countertop edge, at the cabinet edge, or wraps a short return wall.

Write these decisions down. They change both the square footage and how much edge trim you need.

## How to measure a backsplash

Break the backsplash into simple rectangles and measure each one separately. Measure width along the counter and height from the countertop to wherever the tile stops.

![Kitchen backsplash elevation split into three measured sections: a 96 by 18 inch run, a 30 by 36 inch range panel and a 60 by 18 inch run with a 36 by 12 inch window](/images/blog/backsplash-tile-guide/diagram-1.svg)

1. **Measure each section's width** in inches, wall to wall or wall to cabinet end.
2. **Measure each section's height** in at least two places. Countertops and cabinets are rarely perfectly level, so use the larger number.
3. **Multiply width × height** for each section to get square inches, then divide by 144 for square feet.
4. **Subtract large openings** such as windows. Do not subtract outlets or switches.
5. **Add the sections together.**

If you need a refresher on area math for odd shapes, see [how to calculate square footage](/blog/how-to-calculate-square-footage).

### Worked example

Using the layout in the diagram above:

| Section | Width × height | Square inches | Square feet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Left run | 96 in × 18 in | 1,728 | 12.0 |
| Range panel | 30 in × 36 in | 1,080 | 7.5 |
| Right run | 60 in × 18 in | 1,080 | 7.5 |
| Window (subtract) | 36 in × 12 in | −432 | −3.0 |
| **Net area** | | | **24.0** |

With a 10% waste factor for a straight subway layout, you would plan for 24 × 1.10 = **26.4 square feet**, which you round up to whatever whole number of boxes covers it.

## Choosing a waste factor

The waste factor covers cuts, breakage, mistakes and a few spares for future repairs. Backsplashes need more than the floor of the same size because nearly every row ends in a cut and every outlet consumes parts of two to four tiles.

| Layout | Typical waste allowance |
|---|---|
| Straight stack or running bond (subway) | 10% |
| Large-format tile with many cuts | 15% |
| Herringbone or chevron | 15–20% |
| Diagonal layout | 15% |
| Mosaic sheets | 10% (sheets can be cut along joints) |

Also keep at least a few full tiles after the job. Tile lots vary in shade and a discontinued pattern can make a future repair very hard. For a deeper look at why these percentages exist, read our [material waste factor guide](/blog/material-waste-factor-guide).

## Converting square feet to tiles and boxes

Tile is usually sold by the box, and each box lists the square footage it covers. Divide your total (including waste) by the box coverage and round up.

If you need a tile count instead, divide the area of one tile into one square foot (144 square inches), remembering that grout joints slightly increase coverage per tile:

| Tile size | Approx. tiles per sq ft (no joint) |
|---|---|
| 3 × 6 in subway | 8 |
| 2 × 8 in | 9 |
| 4 × 4 in | 9 |
| 4 × 12 in | 3 |
| 12 × 12 in mosaic sheet | 1 sheet |

For the 26.4 square foot example using 3 × 6 inch subway tile: 26.4 × 8 ≈ 212 tiles. If the tile comes in boxes covering 5.5 square feet, you need 26.4 ÷ 5.5 = 4.8, so **5 boxes**.

## Don't forget trim and edges

Exposed tile edges look unfinished, so plan how each one will end. Options include bullnose tiles, pencil trim, or metal or PVC edge profiles. Measure the linear feet of every exposed edge:

- the vertical edge where the backsplash stops at an open counter end,
- the top edge on any wall without upper cabinets,
- the perimeter of a range panel if it sits proud of the field tile.

Add 10% to the trim length for miters and cuts. Profiles usually come in 8-foot lengths, so convert the total and round up.

## Thinset and grout

Backsplash tile is set in thinset mortar (or, for some lightweight wall tiles, a mastic adhesive that the tile manufacturer approves). Coverage depends on the trowel notch size, so use the [Thinset Calculator](/calculators/thinset-calculator) and check the bag label. A small backsplash often needs only one bag.

Grout quantity depends on tile size, tile thickness and joint width. Many backsplash tiles use 1/16 to 1/8 inch joints, which typically call for unsanded grout. The [Grout Calculator](/calculators/grout-calculator) estimates pounds from those dimensions. For a full walkthrough of tile and grout math, see [how to estimate tile and grout](/blog/how-to-estimate-tile-and-grout).

## Plan the layout before you set a tile

A few layout decisions prevent most visual problems:

- **Center on a focal point.** Center the pattern on the range, sink or window so the cut tiles at each end match.
- **Avoid slivers.** If centering leaves a cut smaller than about a third of a tile at a corner, shift the layout by half a tile.
- **Start from a level line.** If the countertop is out of level, set a ledger board or level line for the second row instead of following the counter.
- **Dry-lay a row.** Lay tiles out on the counter with spacers to check how they land around outlets and windows.

The [Tile Layout Calculator](/calculators/tile-layout-calculator) helps you see how many full and cut tiles fall across a given width.

## Surface preparation

Most backsplashes go on painted drywall. The surface should be clean, dry, flat and sound. Glossy paint may need scuffing so adhesive can bond. Remove the outlet and switch cover plates and plan to add box extenders so the devices sit flush with the new tile surface. Behind a cooktop, check the range manufacturer's clearances for combustible materials, especially if you are installing a wood or plastic-backed product.

## Electrical safety note

Turn off the circuit breaker for every outlet and switch in the backsplash area before removing cover plates or working near the boxes, and confirm the power is off with a non-contact voltage tester. Kitchen countertop receptacles generally require GFCI protection. If moving or adding outlets, or if wiring looks damaged, hire a licensed electrician.

## Budgeting the backsplash

Backsplash material costs vary enormously. Basic ceramic subway tile might be a few dollars per square foot, while handmade, glass or natural stone mosaics can cost many times that. Add trim, thinset, grout, sealer (for stone or unglazed tile) and tools such as a wet saw rental. If you plan to hire a pro, small backsplash jobs are often priced with a minimum charge rather than strictly by the square foot. The [Tile Installation Cost Calculator](/calculators/tile-installation-cost-calculator) gives a starting estimate; treat any figure as a typical range that varies by region and date.

## Quick checklist

- Measure each section separately and use the tallest height.
- Subtract windows, not outlets.
- Add 10% for straight layouts, 15–20% for patterns.
- Round up to full boxes and keep spares from the same lot.
- Measure exposed edges for trim.
- Estimate thinset and grout with your actual tile and joint size.
- Shut off power before touching outlets.
