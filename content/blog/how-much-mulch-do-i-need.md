Mulch is one of the best-value improvements you can make to a garden: it holds moisture, suppresses weeds, moderates soil temperature and makes beds look finished. It is also bulky and heavy to move, so ordering the right amount matters. Order too little and you spread it too thin to do any good; too much and you have a pile on the driveway for weeks.

This guide covers how deep to spread mulch, how to measure beds of any shape, how to convert to bags or cubic yards, and the mistakes that harm plants. The [mulch calculator](/calculators/mulch-calculator) does the conversions instantly.

## How deep should mulch be?

Depth drives everything else, so decide it first. These are common recommendations for landscape beds:

| Mulch type | Typical depth | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Shredded hardwood or bark | 2–3 in | Knits together and stays put on gentle slopes |
| Bark nuggets (larger chunks) | 3–4 in | Coarse pieces leave gaps, so go a little deeper |
| Fine or double-shredded mulch | 2 in | Breaks down faster; top up more often |
| Wood chips (arborist chips) | 3–4 in | Good for paths and around trees |
| Pine straw | 2–4 in after settling | Fluffy when spread; settles noticeably |
| Rubber mulch | about 1.5–2 in | Does not break down; check product guidance |
| Decorative stone | 2–3 in | Pair with a fabric barrier to stop it sinking into soil |

More is not better. Mulch deeper than about 4 inches can stop water and oxygen reaching the roots, and it can keep the soil surface wet enough to encourage rot.

### Topping up an existing bed

Organic mulch breaks down over time — that is part of its value — so most beds only need a top-up each year or two. Push a finger or a ruler down to the soil and measure what is left. If you have 1.5 inches and want 3, you only need to add 1.5 inches. Calculating for the full depth every year is one of the most common reasons people over-buy.

## Measuring your beds

You need the **area in square feet** of every bed. Real beds are rarely perfect rectangles, so break them into shapes you can measure:

![Plan of a front yard with a 40 by 5 foot rectangular bed, a curved island bed approximated as 12 by 6 feet, and two 6-foot-diameter tree rings, with areas labelled](/images/blog/how-much-mulch-do-i-need/bed-plan.svg)

- **Rectangular beds:** length × width.
- **Curved or kidney-shaped beds:** measure the length and the average width (take three widths and average them), then multiply.
- **Tree rings:** 3.1416 × radius². For a ring around a large trunk, subtract the trunk area: 3.1416 × (outer radius² − inner radius²). The [tree ring calculator](/calculators/tree-ring-calculator) handles this.
- **Triangular corners:** ½ × base × height.

Our guide on [how to calculate square footage](/blog/how-to-calculate-square-footage) covers more shapes in detail.

## From square feet to cubic yards

Mulch is sold by volume. To convert:

> **Cubic yards = square feet × depth (inches) ÷ 324**

Or, for cubic feet (useful with bags):

> **Cubic feet = square feet × depth (inches) ÷ 12**

The 324 is 27 cubic feet in a yard × 12 inches in a foot. If the arithmetic is new to you, our explainer on [cubic yards](/blog/cubic-yards-explained) walks through it.

| Depth | Area covered by 1 cubic yard | Area covered by one 2 cu ft bag |
|---|---|---|
| 1 in | 324 sq ft | 24 sq ft |
| 2 in | 162 sq ft | 12 sq ft |
| 3 in | 108 sq ft | 8 sq ft |
| 4 in | 81 sq ft | 6 sq ft |

That last column surprises people: a 2 cu ft bag covers only about 8 sq ft at 3 inches deep.

## Worked example: a front yard

From the plan above:

| Area | Calculation | Square feet |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation bed | 40 × 5 | 200 |
| Island bed | 12 × 6 (average width) | 72 |
| Tree ring 1 | 3.1416 × 3² | 28.3 |
| Tree ring 2 | 3.1416 × 3² | 28.3 |
| **Total** | | **328.6** |

**New beds at 3 inches:**

- Cubic yards: 328.6 × 3 ÷ 324 = **3.04 cu yd**
- Add about 5% for settling and uneven ground: **3.2 cu yd** — order 3.25 or 3.5 yards depending on how your supplier sells
- In 2 cu ft bags: 3.04 × 27 = 82.2 cu ft ÷ 2 = 41.1 → **42 bags** (44 with the 5% allowance)

**Annual top-up at 1 inch:**

- 328.6 × 1 ÷ 324 = **1.01 cu yd**, or about 14 bags of 2 cu ft

## Bags or bulk?

| | Bagged mulch | Bulk mulch |
|---|---|---|
| Typical unit | 2 or 3 cu ft bags | Cubic yard, delivered or loaded into your truck |
| Best for | Small beds, top-ups, tight access, mixing colours | New beds, large areas, about 3 yards and up |
| Handling | Easy to carry and store; clean | Shovel and wheelbarrow from a pile |
| Cost | Higher per cubic yard | Lower per cubic yard, plus delivery |
| Waste | Plastic bags | No packaging |

Prices vary widely by region, mulch type and season. As an illustration only: if 2 cu ft bags cost $4.50 each, 42 bags is about $190. If bulk mulch costs $40 per yard with a $75 delivery fee, 3.5 yards is about $215 — similar, but with spare mulch and no bags to dispose of. At larger quantities bulk usually pulls ahead clearly. Check your local prices and plug them into the [landscape cost calculator](/calculators/landscape-cost-calculator).

If you are collecting bulk mulch yourself, a cubic yard of wood mulch is light compared with soil or gravel, but it still fills a full-size pickup bed — check your bed volume before you go.

## How to spread mulch properly

![Two trees side by side: on the left mulch piled against the trunk in a volcano shape, marked as wrong; on the right a flat 2 to 3 inch ring of mulch with a few inches of clear space around the trunk flare, marked as right](/images/blog/how-much-mulch-do-i-need/tree-mulch.svg)

1. **Weed first.** Pull or treat weeds before mulching; mulch suppresses new weeds but does not reliably kill established ones.
2. **Edge the bed.** A clean edge keeps mulch in and grass out. The [edging calculator](/calculators/edging-calculator) estimates lengths.
3. **Water the soil** if it is dry, so moisture is held in rather than kept out.
4. **Spread evenly** to your target depth, using a rake to level it. Check depth in several places.
5. **Keep it off trunks and stems.** Leave a few inches of clear space around tree trunks and shrub stems. Mulch piled against bark ("mulch volcanoes") holds moisture against it, which can lead to rot, disease and pest problems.
6. **Keep it back from the house.** Leave a gap between mulch and siding, and do not bury weep holes or the top of the foundation. In areas with termites, many pest professionals recommend keeping organic mulch a little further from the foundation.

## Choosing a mulch type

- **Shredded hardwood:** A good all-rounder; mats together and resists washing away.
- **Pine bark nuggets:** Long-lasting and attractive, but can float away in heavy rain.
- **Dyed mulch:** Holds colour for a season or so; choose products from reputable suppliers.
- **Pine straw:** Light, easy to spread and good on slopes; sold in bales whose coverage varies by bale size, so ask the supplier how many square feet a bale covers.
- **Compost:** A great soil improver but not a long-lasting weed barrier on its own. See [topsoil vs garden soil vs compost](/blog/topsoil-vs-garden-soil-vs-compost) for how it differs from other soil products.
- **Stone:** Permanent and tidy, but it heats up in sun and does not feed the soil. Our [gravel types guide](/blog/gravel-types-guide) compares decorative stone options.

## Quick reference

- Most beds: 2–3 inches of mulch; no more than about 4 inches
- Cubic yards = sq ft × depth (in) ÷ 324
- 1 cubic yard = 13.5 bags of 2 cu ft
- Measure existing mulch and only top up the difference
- Keep mulch off trunks, stems and siding
