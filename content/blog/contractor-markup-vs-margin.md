"Markup" and "margin" are used interchangeably in a lot of conversations about contractor pricing, but they aren't the same number. Mixing them up is one of the most common ways small contractors underprice jobs, and it's also why homeowners sometimes think a bid includes far more profit than it really does.

This guide explains both terms, gives the formulas and a conversion table, and walks through examples from both sides of the table. The [Contractor Markup Calculator](/calculators/contractor-markup-calculator) converts between them instantly.

## The definitions

Start with three numbers:

- **Cost:** what the job costs the contractor in materials, labor and subcontractors.
- **Price:** what the customer pays.
- **Gross profit:** price minus cost.

Then:

- **Markup** = gross profit ÷ **cost**
- **Margin** = gross profit ÷ **price**

Same gross profit, different denominators. Because price is always bigger than cost (when a job is profitable), the margin percentage is always smaller than the markup percentage.

## A simple example

A contractor's cost for a job is $40,000 and the price is $50,000.

- Gross profit: $50,000 − $40,000 = **$10,000**
- Markup: $10,000 ÷ $40,000 = **25%**
- Margin: $10,000 ÷ $50,000 = **20%**

Both statements are true at the same time: this job has a 25% markup and a 20% margin.

## Conversion formulas

**Markup to margin:** margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup)

**Margin to markup:** markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin)

**Price from cost and markup:** price = cost × (1 + markup)

**Price from cost and target margin:** price = cost ÷ (1 − margin)

## The classic mistake

Suppose a remodeler knows from their books that overhead runs about 15% of revenue, and they want a 10% net profit. They need a **25% gross margin**. If they mistakenly add a 25% *markup*:

- Cost: $40,000
- Price with 25% markup: $40,000 × 1.25 = **$50,000**
- Actual margin: $10,000 ÷ $50,000 = **20%**

That's 5 percentage points short of the 25% margin they needed. Overhead (15% of $50,000 = $7,500) leaves only **$2,500** of net profit, or 5% instead of 10%.

To actually hit a 25% margin:

- Price = $40,000 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = **$53,333**
- Markup required: $13,333 ÷ $40,000 = **33.3%**

That $3,333 difference on a single job is pure profit lost to a terminology mix-up. Over a year of similar jobs, it can be the difference between a healthy business and one that's always short of cash.

## Why margin is the better planning number

Business expenses such as overhead and target profit are usually tracked as a percentage of **revenue** (sales). Since margin is also a percentage of revenue, it lines up directly:

**Gross margin needed = overhead % of revenue + net profit % of revenue**

Once you know the margin you need, convert it to a markup to apply to your costs. Markup is the convenient number to *apply*; margin is the right number to *plan* with.

## "Overhead and profit" and "10 and 10"

You may hear contractors describe pricing as "10 and 10," meaning 10% overhead and 10% profit. It's important to know how it's applied:

- **Added together:** cost × 1.20 → a 20% markup, which is a **16.7% margin**.
- **Compounded:** cost × 1.10 × 1.10 = cost × 1.21 → a 21% markup, about a **17.4% margin**.

Neither gives a 20% margin. Whether "10 and 10" is enough depends entirely on the business's real overhead, which varies with company size, insurance, vehicles, office space, staff and how much unpaid time goes into estimating and sales.

## What overhead actually covers

For homeowners reading a bid, it helps to understand that markup isn't all profit. Gross profit must cover the costs of running the business that can't be charged to one job directly, such as:

- General liability and workers' compensation insurance
- Licensing, bonding and permits administration
- Vehicles, fuel and tool replacement
- Office rent, software, phones and accounting
- Time spent estimating jobs that aren't won
- Warranty callbacks and fixing problems after completion
- Supervision and project management

A contractor who doesn't cover these consistently may cut corners, delay jobs or go out of business mid-project, none of which is good for the customer.

## Reading a bid as a homeowner

Markups vary widely by trade, region, job size and risk, and there is no single "correct" percentage. Instead of fixating on the markup, focus on:

- **Scope clarity.** Does the bid describe exactly what's included?
- **Allowances.** Are allowances for fixtures, tile or cabinets realistic?
- **Change-order pricing.** How will extra work be priced, and will the same markup apply?
- **Payment schedule.** Payments tied to completed milestones protect both sides.
- **Like-for-like comparison.** Compare bids with the same scope, materials and allowances.

If a bid is much lower than others, ask what's missing. Sometimes it's a smaller markup, but often it's a smaller scope or lower allowances. ## Worked example: pricing a deck job

A deck builder estimates these job costs:

| Cost item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Lumber, hardware and fasteners | $9,200 |
| Labor (crew wages and burden) | $7,800 |
| Permit and disposal | $600 |
| Concrete footings subcontractor | $1,400 |
| **Total job cost** | **$19,000** |

The builder's books show overhead running about 18% of revenue and the target net profit is 10%. Required gross margin: 18% + 10% = **28%**.

- Price: $19,000 ÷ (1 − 0.28) = **$26,389**
- Gross profit: $26,389 − $19,000 = **$7,389**
- Markup equivalent: $7,389 ÷ $19,000 = **38.9%**

If the builder had simply applied a 28% markup, the price would have been $24,320, about $2,069 less, and the actual margin would have been only 21.9%. ## Markup on change orders

Change orders are where markup confusion often turns into disputes. Before work starts, both sides should agree in writing how added work will be priced. Common approaches include:

- **Cost plus the same markup** used in the original bid, with receipts for materials.
- **Fixed unit prices** agreed in advance, such as a price per sheet of replacement roof decking or per linear foot of replaced subfloor.
- **Time and materials** at stated hourly rates plus materials and an agreed percentage.

For contractors, the key is that change orders still carry overhead: someone has to price, order, schedule and supervise the extra work. Applying the margin-based markup from your planning (not a lower "favor" rate) keeps change work from eroding the job's profit. For homeowners, a clear change-order clause means no surprises when hidden damage turns up behind a wall.

## Quick reference

- Markup = profit ÷ cost. Margin = profit ÷ price.
- Margin is always smaller than markup for the same job.
- Plan with margin, apply with markup.
- Margin needed = overhead % + net profit %.
- Markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin).
- Markup isn't all profit; overhead comes out of it first.
