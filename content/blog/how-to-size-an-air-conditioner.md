Buying an air conditioner by guesswork tends to go wrong in one of two ways. Too small and it runs flat out all afternoon without ever catching up. Too big and it blasts cold air, shuts off, and leaves the room clammy. The right size depends on how much heat your space gains, and the starting point for that is a number every AC is labeled with: BTU per hour.

This guide covers BTU basics, the room-size chart used for window and portable units, the adjustments that matter, and why central systems need a proper load calculation. The [AC BTU Calculator](/calculators/ac-btu-calculator) applies the same rules to your room.

## What BTU means for air conditioners

A British thermal unit (BTU) is the energy needed to raise one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. AC capacity is expressed in BTU per hour, usually shortened to "BTU." Central systems are often described in tons, where:

- **1 ton = 12,000 BTU per hour**
- 2 tons = 24,000 BTU/h, 3 tons = 36,000 BTU/h, and so on.

The term comes from the heat absorbed by melting one ton of ice over 24 hours.

## Step 1: Measure the room

Multiply length by width to get square feet. For an L-shaped room, split it into rectangles and add them; the [Room Area Calculator](/calculators/room-area-calculator) handles this, and our guide on [how to calculate square footage](/blog/how-to-calculate-square-footage) explains the method. If two rooms are open to each other with no door, treat them as one space.

## Step 2: Find the base capacity

ENERGY STAR publishes a sizing chart for room air conditioners. It assumes typical ceiling heights (around 8 feet) and average insulation:

|---|---|
| 100 up to 150 | 5,000 |
| 150 up to 250 | 6,000 |
| 250 up to 300 | 7,000 |
| 300 up to 350 | 8,000 |
| 350 up to 400 | 9,000 |
| 400 up to 450 | 10,000 |
| 450 up to 550 | 12,000 |
| 550 up to 700 | 14,000 |
| 700 up to 1,000 | 18,000 |
| 1,000 up to 1,200 | 21,000 |
| 1,200 up to 1,400 | 23,000 |
| 1,400 up to 1,500 | 24,000 |
| 1,500 up to 2,000 | 30,000 |
| 2,000 up to 2,500 | 34,000 |

## Step 3: Adjust for your conditions

ENERGY STAR recommends these adjustments to the base number:

- **Heavily shaded room:** reduce capacity by 10%.
- **Very sunny room:** increase capacity by 10%.
- **More than two people regularly occupy the room:** add 600 BTU for each additional person.
- **The unit is used in a kitchen:** increase capacity by 4,000 BTU.

Other factors the simple chart doesn't capture but you should consider:

- **High or vaulted ceilings** add volume. A 12-foot ceiling holds 50% more air than an 8-foot one.
- **Poor insulation or leaky windows** increase heat gain.
- **Top-floor rooms** under a hot roof gain more heat than ground-floor rooms.
- **Heat-producing equipment** such as gaming PCs or a home gym adds load.
- **Climate.** A room in a hot, humid region works harder than the same room in a mild one.

Where these apply, lean toward the upper end of the size band rather than jumping two sizes.

## Worked example

A living room measures 14 × 18 feet with two large south-facing windows, and three people usually watch TV there in the evening.

1. Area: 14 × 18 = **252 sq ft** → base capacity **7,000 BTU** (250–300 band).
2. Very sunny: 7,000 × 1.10 = **7,700 BTU**.
3. Three people is one more than two: 7,700 + 600 = **8,300 BTU**.

The nearest common sizes are 8,000 and 10,000 BTU. An 8,000 BTU unit is slightly under the adjusted figure; many buyers would choose 8,000 if the windows have good blinds, or step up modestly if the room also has a high ceiling. Choosing a 12,000 BTU unit "to be safe" would risk the short-cycling problems described next.

## Why bigger isn't better

Air conditioners do two jobs: lower the temperature (sensible cooling) and remove moisture (latent cooling). Moisture comes out as air passes over the cold coil, and that takes run time.

An oversized unit hits the thermostat setpoint quickly and shuts off before it has removed much moisture. The result:

- The room feels cold and damp at the same time.
- The unit cycles on and off frequently, which increases wear and wastes energy at startup.
- Temperatures swing more between cycles.

An undersized unit has the opposite problem: it runs continuously on hot days and may never reach the setpoint. A correctly sized unit runs longer, steadier cycles.

## Window vs portable vs mini-split

| Type | Notes |
|---|---|
| Window unit | Usually the most efficient per dollar for one room; needs a suitable window. |
| Portable unit | Easy to move, but the exhaust hose and room-air leakage reduce effective cooling. Compare models by their published seasonally adjusted cooling capacity (SACC) where available, rather than the larger legacy BTU figure. |
| Ductless mini-split | Efficient and quiet; typically professionally installed and sized. |
| Central air | Serves the whole house through ducts; must be sized with a load calculation. |

## Central air: why you need a Manual J

The square-foot chart above is for room units. For central air and heat pumps, the industry standard is a load calculation using **ACCA Manual J**, published by the Air Conditioning Contractors of America. It accounts for:

- wall, ceiling and floor insulation levels,
- window area, orientation, glass type and shading,
- air leakage and duct location,
- local design temperatures and humidity,
- occupants and internal heat gains.

Many building codes require a Manual J (or equivalent) when replacing or installing equipment, and the equipment selection that follows uses ACCA Manual S, with ductwork designed to Manual D. Be cautious of any contractor who sizes a replacement by matching the old unit's tonnage or by a quick square-foot rule. If you've added insulation, replaced windows or sealed ducts since the old system was installed, the house may need less capacity than before. Our [attic insulation guide](/blog/attic-insulation-guide) covers one of the upgrades that often changes the load.

## Electrical safety note

Larger room air conditioners can draw significant current and some require a 230/240-volt receptacle. Check the unit's plug type and amperage before buying. Plug room ACs directly into a properly rated wall outlet; manufacturers generally warn against extension cords and power strips because of overheating risk. If the outlet is loose, discolored or the breaker trips, have an electrician check the circuit. Install window units with the manufacturer's brackets so they can't fall, especially above ground floor. Central systems and mini-splits involve refrigerant and high-voltage wiring, which should be handled by licensed professionals.

## Quick checklist

- Measure the room in square feet.
- Use the ENERGY STAR chart for the base BTU.
- Adjust for sun, shade, occupants and kitchens.
- Consider ceiling height, insulation and top-floor heat.
- Don't oversize; choose the nearest size that meets the adjusted load.
- Use a Manual J calculation for central air and heat pumps.
- Check voltage and plug type, and never use an extension cord.
