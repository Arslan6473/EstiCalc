Topsoil, garden soil and compost are often stacked side by side at the garden center, and they look similar enough that it's tempting to grab whichever bag is cheapest. But they do different jobs. Using topsoil where you needed compost won't feed your plants much, and filling a raised bed with nothing but compost can leave it sinking and nutrient-imbalanced by midsummer.

This guide explains what each product is, where each belongs, how deep to apply it, and how to work out the quantity. ## What each one is

### Topsoil

Topsoil is the upper layer of natural soil, a mix of mineral particles (sand, silt and clay) with some organic matter. Commercial topsoil is dug from sites being developed or farmed and is usually **screened** to remove rocks, roots and clods.

- **Job:** bulk fill and structure. It fills low spots, builds grade, creates a base for new lawns and bulks out raised-bed mixes.
- **Watch out for:** quality varies hugely. Some "topsoil" is mostly subsoil or heavy clay, and it may carry weed seeds. There's no single national standard for what can be sold as topsoil.

### Compost

Compost is decomposed organic material such as yard trimmings, food scraps or manure, broken down until it's dark, crumbly and earthy-smelling.

- **Job:** soil amendment. It improves structure (loosening clay, helping sandy soil hold water), feeds soil life and supplies slow-release nutrients.
- **Watch out for:** unfinished compost that is still hot or smells sour can harm plants. Some composts are high in salts. Compost alone shrinks considerably as it continues to break down.

### Garden soil

"Garden soil" is a marketing category rather than a defined material. It's usually topsoil blended with compost or other organic matter, sometimes with added fertilizer.

- **Job:** ready-to-use mix for planting beds and raised beds, often blended with the native soil.
- **Watch out for:** read the bag. Ingredients and ratios vary between brands.

### Potting mix (for comparison)

Potting mix is a soilless blend, typically peat or coir with bark, perlite or vermiculite. It's for containers only.

## Which one for your project?

### Filling low spots and regrading

Use **screened topsoil**. Compost would shrink and leave the low spot again. Fill in layers, firming each one, and slope soil away from the house foundation.

### Starting a new lawn (seed or sod)

Spread **topsoil** over compacted or poor ground, often 4–6 inches, and optionally blend in compost to improve structure. Rake level and lightly firm before seeding or laying sod. The [Sod Calculator](/calculators/sod-calculator) and [Grass Seed Calculator](/calculators/grass-seed-calculator) cover the next step.

### Improving an existing lawn

Top-dress with a thin layer of **compost**, about 1/4 to 1/2 inch, ideally after core aeration so it falls into the holes. Thicker layers can smother grass.

### Preparing an in-ground vegetable or flower bed

Spread **2–3 inches of compost** and work it into the top 6–8 inches of native soil. If the soil is very poor or shallow, add topsoil or a garden-soil blend as well.

### Filling a raised bed

Use a **blend**. Topsoil alone can compact; compost alone shrinks and may be too rich. Many gardeners use a mix of screened topsoil and compost in roughly equal parts or with a topsoil majority, or buy a pre-blended raised-bed mix. Some also add an aeration material such as perlite or fine bark for drainage. Your local cooperative extension service often publishes a recommended mix for the region.

### Containers

Use **potting mix**, not garden soil or topsoil.

## Test before you amend

A soil test tells you pH and nutrient levels so you add what's actually needed. University cooperative extension offices in the US offer low-cost soil testing. Testing before adding large amounts of compost or fertilizer avoids overdoing phosphorus or salts.

## How much to buy

### Step 1: Calculate cubic feet

Length (ft) × width (ft) × depth (ft). Divide depth in inches by 12 to convert to feet.

### Step 2: Convert to cubic yards or bags

- Cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27. See [cubic yards explained](/blog/cubic-yards-explained).
- Bags = cubic feet ÷ bag size (bags list cubic feet on the front).

| Bag size | Bags per cubic yard |
|---|---|
| 0.75 cu ft | 36 |
| 1 cu ft | 27 |
| 1.5 cu ft | 18 |
| 2 cu ft | 13.5 |

### Step 3: Allow for settling

Loose soil and compost settle after watering and as organic matter breaks down. For raised beds, buying around 10–20% extra and topping up later is common. See the [material waste factor guide](/blog/material-waste-factor-guide) for how allowances work.

### Worked example: two raised beds plus a lawn top-dress

**Raised beds:** two beds, each 4 × 8 feet and 12 inches deep, filled with a 60/40 topsoil-to-compost blend.

- Volume per bed: 4 × 8 × 1 = **32 cu ft**
- Two beds: **64 cu ft**
- Add 15% for settling: 64 × 1.15 = **73.6 cu ft** ≈ **2.7 cu yd**
- Topsoil (60%): 73.6 × 0.6 = 44.2 cu ft ≈ **1.6 cu yd**
- Compost (40%): 73.6 × 0.4 = 29.4 cu ft ≈ **1.1 cu yd**

In 1.5 cu ft bags, that rounds up to about 50 bags in total, which is why bulk delivery usually makes sense at this size.

**Lawn top-dress:** 2,000 sq ft of lawn at 1/4 inch of compost.

- Depth: 0.25 ÷ 12 = 0.0208 ft
- Volume: 2,000 × 0.0208 = **41.7 cu ft** ≈ **1.5 cu yd**

## Bulk vs bagged

Bagged products are convenient for small jobs and are usually more consistent. Bulk is typically much cheaper per yard for larger jobs. When buying bulk:

- Ask whether the topsoil is screened and where it came from.
- Look at or squeeze a sample: it should crumble, not form a sticky ball (heavy clay) or fall apart like beach sand.
- Ask whether the compost is finished and screened, and what feedstock it's made from.
- Have a tarp ready if the pile will sit on a driveway or lawn.

Mulch is the other bulk material most gardens need; [how much mulch do I need](/blog/how-much-mulch-do-i-need) covers depth and coverage.

## Safety note

Wear gloves when handling soil and compost, and avoid breathing dust from dry bagged products by dampening them first and wearing a dust mask if needed. Wash hands afterward, especially before eating. Call 811 before digging deep beds or regrading so buried utilities can be marked. A cubic yard of moist soil is heavy, so pace wheelbarrow work and don't overload.

## Quick checklist

- Topsoil for bulk, grading and new lawns.
- Compost to improve soil and feed plants.
- Garden soil or a blend for beds; potting mix for containers.
- Test your soil before heavy amending.
- Calculate cubic feet, then yards or bags.
- Add 10–20% for settling in raised beds.
