Picking a paint colour gets all the attention, but the **sheen** — how shiny the dried paint is — affects how the room looks, how well the walls stand up to cleaning, and how visible every dent and patch will be. Choose the wrong one and a beautiful colour can look patchy on a wall full of old repairs, or a hallway can be scuffed beyond saving within a year.

This guide explains what each sheen does, where to use it, and the trade-offs between them. Sheen does not usually change how much paint you need much, so once you've chosen, the [paint calculator](/calculators/paint-calculator) will size the job.

## What "sheen" actually means

Sheen is the amount of light a dried paint film reflects. Paint manufacturers measure it with a glossmeter, which shines light at the surface at a set angle and measures how much bounces back, in "gloss units". Higher numbers mean shinier paint.

Shinier paints have a smoother, tighter film with proportionally more binder (resin) and less of the flattening agents that scatter light. That is why gloss and durability tend to rise together — and why shine also highlights texture: a smooth reflective film shows every bump, roller mark and patch, especially when light hits the wall at a low angle.

## The sheens, from flattest to shiniest

The Master Painters Institute (MPI) publishes a widely referenced gloss scale. The ranges below are approximate gloss units measured at a 60° angle on that scale; individual brands' products vary, and their names do not always line up with these categories.

| Sheen | Approx. 60° gloss | Look | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flat | 0–5 | No shine, velvety | Best at hiding flaws; easy touch-ups | Least washable; marks easily |
| Matte / velvet | up to about 10 | Very low shine | Hides flaws; many modern "washable matte" lines resist scrubbing well | Varies widely by brand |
| Eggshell | about 10–25 | Soft, low lustre | Good balance of hiding and cleanability | Shows more flaws than flat |
| Satin | about 20–35 | Pearl-like glow | Washable, durable, handles humidity | Shows roller marks and patches |
| Semi-gloss | about 35–70 | Clearly shiny | Very durable and scrubbable; resists moisture | Highlights every imperfection |
| Gloss / high-gloss | about 70 and above | Mirror-like | Hardest, most washable; dramatic look | Demands near-perfect prep |

### Flat and matte

Flat paint scatters light, which makes walls look uniform even when they are not. That is why ceilings are almost always flat — ceilings are lit at a grazing angle by windows and fixtures, which exaggerates every seam. Flat is also forgiving for touch-ups: a dab of the same paint usually blends in.

The weakness is cleaning. Traditional flat paints can burnish (turn shiny) or lose pigment when scrubbed. Many manufacturers now sell "washable" or "scrubbable" matte finishes that close much of that gap, and these are popular for living rooms, dining rooms and bedrooms.

### Eggshell

Named for the soft lustre of an eggshell, this is the default wall sheen in many homes. It wipes clean from fingerprints and light marks better than flat while still hiding minor wall flaws reasonably well. Good for living rooms, bedrooms, dining rooms and family rooms.

### Satin

Satin has a noticeable glow and holds up to more frequent cleaning and moisture. It suits kitchens, bathrooms, laundry rooms, hallways, stairwells and kids' rooms. The trade-off is that patches, drywall seams and uneven rolling show more, and touch-ups can "flash" (look shinier or duller than the surrounding wall). When touching up satin, it often looks better to repaint a whole wall corner to corner.

### Semi-gloss

The classic choice for **interior trim, doors, window sills and cabinets**, where hands, shoes and vacuum cleaners make contact. It is easy to wipe down and resists moisture, so it is also used in some bathrooms. On walls it can look harsh and will show every flaw, so it is rarely used there except in utility spaces.

### Gloss and high-gloss

The hardest, most cleanable finish, used for front doors, furniture, railings, cabinets and accent trim where a lacquer-like look is wanted. It needs excellent prep — any sanding scratch, brush mark or dust nib will be visible — and is often applied in several thin coats.

## Which sheen where: a room-by-room guide

| Surface | Common choice | Alternatives |
|---|---|---|
| Ceilings | Flat | Matte; satin or semi-gloss in a steamy bathroom if the product is suited to it |
| Living room, dining room | Eggshell or washable matte | Flat for a very smooth, low-traffic room |
| Bedrooms | Eggshell or matte | Satin for kids' rooms |
| Kitchen walls | Satin or eggshell | Washable matte from a scrubbable line |
| Bathroom walls | Satin | Semi-gloss; or paints formulated for bathrooms |
| Hallways and stairwells | Satin or eggshell | — |
| Interior trim and doors | Semi-gloss | Satin for a softer look; gloss for drama |
| Kitchen and bath cabinets | Semi-gloss or satin enamel | Gloss for a high-end lacquered look |
| Exterior siding | Flat or satin | Low-lustre to hide surface texture |
| Exterior trim, shutters, front door | Semi-gloss or gloss | — |

Ventilation matters more than sheen in bathrooms: an exhaust fan that clears steam does more to prevent peeling and mildew than any finish.

## Practical tips

- **Test before committing.** Paint a sample board or large test patch and look at it in morning and evening light, and with a light shining across it at a low angle.
- **Prep to match the sheen.** The glossier the paint, the smoother the surface must be. Under semi-gloss or gloss, sand patches carefully and consider a skim coat on rough walls — our [drywall estimating guide](/blog/drywall-estimating-guide) explains the Level 5 finish recommended under glossy paint.
- **Change sheen carefully.** Scuff-sand or degloss shiny surfaces before painting over them, and use a bonding primer where needed; the [primer calculator](/calculators/primer-calculator) sizes the primer coat.
- **Keep sheens consistent.** Within one room, using the same wall sheen throughout avoids visible differences where walls meet.
- **Record what you used.** Write the brand, product line, colour and sheen inside a closet door or on the lid, so touch-ups match.

## Worked example: painting a 12 × 14 ft bedroom

A typical plan uses three sheens:

| Surface | Sheen | Area | Coats | Paint needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Walls | Eggshell | 366 sq ft (after doors and windows) | 2 | 366 × 2 ÷ 350 × 1.10 = 2.3 gal → 3 gal |
| Ceiling | Flat | 168 sq ft | 2 | 168 × 2 ÷ 350 × 1.10 = 1.06 gal → 1 gal plus a quart, or 2 gal |
| Trim and door | Semi-gloss | about 60 sq ft (baseboard, casings, both sides of door) | 2 | 60 × 2 ÷ 350 × 1.10 = 0.38 gal → 2 quarts |

Coverage is based on 350 sq ft per gallon; use the figure on your product's label. Trim figures are rough because they depend on the profile of your mouldings. If you are repainting the same white ceiling, one coat may do, which halves that line. Our guide on [how much paint do I need](/blog/how-much-paint-do-i-need) explains each step of this calculation, and the [number of coats calculator](/calculators/number-of-coats-calculator) helps decide between one coat and two.

## Quick reference

- Flat and matte: hide flaws best; least washable (except "washable matte" lines)
- Eggshell: the all-round wall choice
- Satin: kitchens, bathrooms, hallways and kids' rooms
- Semi-gloss: trim, doors and cabinets
- Gloss: front doors, furniture and statement pieces
- More shine = more durability, but more visible flaws
