Laminate and luxury vinyl plank (LVP) look almost identical on the showroom floor. Both imitate wood, both click together as a floating floor, and both are popular DIY projects. Underneath, though, they are very different products, and that difference decides which one belongs in your kitchen, basement or bedroom.

This guide compares them on the factors that matter in daily life — water, wear, comfort, noise, installation and cost — and then walks through a budget example. ## What each floor is made of

![Cross-section of a laminate plank showing wear layer, printed decor layer, HDF core and backing layer, next to a rigid-core vinyl plank showing wear layer, printed film, vinyl or SPC core and attached pad](/images/blog/laminate-vs-vinyl-plank-flooring/plank-layers.svg)

**Laminate** is a wood-based product. A high-density fibreboard (HDF) core is sandwiched between a balancing backer and a printed photographic layer, with a hard, clear wear layer on top. Because the core is compressed wood fibre, it can swell if water soaks into the joints or edges.

**Luxury vinyl plank** is plastic-based. It has a clear wear layer and a printed film over a vinyl core. Most click-together LVP sold today is "rigid core", which comes in two main types:

- **SPC (stone plastic composite):** a dense core of limestone powder and PVC. Thin, stiff and dent resistant.
- **WPC (wood plastic composite):** a foamed core that is thicker, a little softer and warmer underfoot.

There is also flexible glue-down vinyl plank, which is thinner and commonly used in commercial spaces.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Laminate | Vinyl plank (rigid core) |
|---|---|---|
| Core | HDF (wood fibre) | PVC/limestone (SPC) or foamed PVC (WPC) |
| Water | Water resistant at best; core can swell | Core is unaffected by water |
| Scratch resistance | Excellent; very hard top layer | Good; depends on wear layer thickness |
| Dent resistance | Good | Very good (SPC), good (WPC) |
| Feel underfoot | Firm, more "wood-like" | Slightly softer and warmer, especially WPC |
| Noise | Can sound hollow without good underlayment | Generally quieter |
| Realism | Often very realistic texture and depth | Very realistic on better products |
| Sunlight and heat | Stable | Can expand in strong sun; check limits |
| Durability rating | AC rating (AC1–AC5) | Wear layer in mil (e.g. 12 mil, 20 mil) |
| Refinishing | Not possible | Not possible |
| DIY installation | Click-lock; cuts with saw, dusty | Click-lock; many score-and-snap with a utility knife |

## Water: the deciding factor for many rooms

This is the biggest practical difference. A spilled glass of water wiped up within a few minutes will not hurt either floor. Standing water is another story. If water sits on laminate joints, it can wick into the HDF core and cause the edges to swell or "peak" — damage that does not reverse when the floor dries.

Rigid-core vinyl will not swell from water, which is why it is marketed as waterproof. Be careful with that word, though: the *plank* is waterproof, but the *floor* is not a sealed membrane. Water can run through the seams and around the edges, and get trapped on the subfloor below, where it can lead to mould or damage to a wooden subfloor. A major leak still means pulling up the floor.

Some newer laminates are sold as water-resistant or waterproof for a stated number of hours of surface water. These can be good options for kitchens, but read the warranty conditions carefully — they often require specific installation details such as sealed perimeters.

## Durability: AC ratings vs wear layers

The two products describe toughness differently.

**Laminate** uses AC (abrasion class) ratings from a European testing standard:

| AC rating | Typical intended use |
|---|---|
| AC1 | Light residential (bedrooms, guest rooms) |
| AC2 | General residential (living and dining rooms) |
| AC3 | Heavy residential, light commercial — a solid choice for busy homes |
| AC4 | General commercial |
| AC5 | Heavy commercial |

**Vinyl plank** quotes wear layer thickness in mils (thousandths of an inch). Around 12 mil is a common residential grade, while 20 mil and above is often sold for heavier traffic, pets and light commercial use. Thin wear layers (6–8 mil) are budget products best kept to low-traffic rooms.

Laminate generally wins on scratch resistance — pet claws and grit are less likely to mark it. Vinyl tends to shrug off impacts better, and it does not chip at the edges the way laminate can if something heavy is dropped on a seam.

## Comfort and noise

Laminate's hard surface can produce a hollow, clicky sound when walked on, particularly with hard-soled shoes. A quality underlayment makes a noticeable difference, and if you live in an apartment or are installing on an upper floor, check building rules for required sound ratings.

Vinyl is a little softer and warmer to the touch, and WPC in particular feels more cushioned. Many rigid-core vinyl planks come with an attached pad, so you may not need a separate underlayment — only a vapour barrier over concrete if the manufacturer calls for one. ## Installation differences

Both are usually installed as floating floors with click-lock joints, and both need:

- A flat, clean, dry subfloor (manufacturers specify a flatness tolerance)
- An expansion gap around the perimeter, hidden by baseboard or quarter round
- Transition strips at doorways and where the floor meets other materials
- No fixed cabinets or kitchen islands pinning the floor down

The main differences are cutting and climate. Laminate needs a saw for most cuts and produces fine dust; work outdoors or use dust collection. Many rigid vinyl planks can be scored with a utility knife and snapped, which is faster and cleaner indoors. Laminate is more sensitive to humidity, while some vinyl planks can expand in strong direct sunlight, so sunrooms and rooms with large south- or west-facing glass need a product rated for it.

## Cost

Both span a wide price range, and prices vary by region, brand and date. As a rough guide to typical US retail pricing:

| Item | Laminate | Vinyl plank |
|---|---|---|
| Budget material | about $1–$2 per sq ft | about $1.50–$2.50 per sq ft |
| Mid-range material | about $2–$4 per sq ft | about $2.50–$4.50 per sq ft |
| Premium material | $4 per sq ft and up | $4.50 per sq ft and up |
| Professional installation | often $2–$5 per sq ft | often $2–$5 per sq ft |

Installation quotes depend on prep work (removing old floors, levelling, moving appliances), trim, stairs and local labour rates. Get itemised quotes and compare like with like. ## Worked example: 400 sq ft of open-plan living space

Suppose you are flooring a 400 sq ft living and dining area and comparing two mid-range products:

- Laminate at $2.50 per sq ft, needing a separate underlayment at $0.40 per sq ft
- Rigid-core vinyl at $3.25 per sq ft with an attached pad

**Material with 10% waste:** 400 × 1.10 = 440 sq ft for both.

| | Laminate | Vinyl plank |
|---|---|---|
| Planks | 440 × $2.50 = $1,100 | 440 × $3.25 = $1,430 |
| Underlayment | 400 × $0.40 = $160 | $0 (attached) |
| Material subtotal | **$1,260** | **$1,430** |
| Labour at $3.00 per sq ft (if hired) | $1,200 | $1,200 |
| Total installed | **$2,460** | **$2,630** |

In this example the gap is about $170 — small enough that durability and water resistance should drive the decision, not price. Trim, transitions and removal of the old floor are extra for both.

## Which one for which room?

| Room | Better fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Full bathroom, laundry room | Vinyl | Splashes, leaks, humidity |
| Basement on concrete | Vinyl | Moisture risk from the slab |
| Kitchen | Vinyl, or water-resistant laminate | Spills are frequent; check warranty |
| Living room, bedrooms | Either | Laminate's realism and scratch resistance shine here |
| Homes with large dogs | Laminate (high AC) or 20+ mil vinyl | Claws vs water bowls — weigh your priority |
| Sunroom | Product rated for heat and sun | Check temperature limits |
| Upper-floor apartment | Either, with acoustic underlayment | Building sound rules |

## The bottom line

Choose **vinyl plank** wherever water is a realistic risk — bathrooms, laundry rooms, basements and most kitchens. Choose **laminate** for dry living spaces where you want maximum scratch resistance and a firm, wood-like feel, often at a slightly lower material price. In many homes the best answer is both: vinyl in the wet rooms, laminate in the rest.
