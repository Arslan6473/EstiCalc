A fence is a straightforward project on paper — posts in the ground, rails between them, boards on the rails — but the planning decides whether it goes in smoothly. Posts in the wrong place, sections that don't divide evenly, a gate that sags, or a fence a few inches over the property line are all problems that start before the first hole is dug.

This guide walks through planning a typical wood fence from layout to materials list, and then works through a 100-ft example. The [fence calculator](/calculators/fence-calculator) produces a materials list from your measurements.

## Before you measure: rules and boundaries

Check these first, because they can change the design:

- **Property line.** Know exactly where it is. A survey or the original plat, with boundary markers located, is the reliable answer; many people set their fence a little inside the line to be safe.
- **Permits and height limits.** Many towns require a permit and limit fence height (often lower in front yards than back yards), and some have setback rules near sidewalks and corners.
- **HOA rules.** Homeowners associations often specify style, height, material and which side faces out.
- **Pool barriers.** Fences around pools usually have specific height, gap and self-closing gate requirements.
- **Utilities.** In the US, call **811** a few days before you dig so buried lines are marked. Hitting a gas or electric line is dangerous and expensive.

## Step 1: Lay out the fence line

Drive a stake at each end and each corner, and run a tight string line between them. Measure the length of each straight run. Mark where gates will go and decide how wide each one needs to be — a 3–4 ft walk gate is typical; mower and wheelbarrow access may need wider, and driveway gates are often double gates.

## Step 2: Space the posts

Most wood fences use posts **6 to 8 ft apart, on centre**. Eight feet is popular because rails are commonly sold in 8-ft lengths, but in exposed, windy locations or with heavy boards, 6 ft gives a stiffer fence.

For each straight run:

> **Sections = run length ÷ maximum spacing, rounded up**
> **Posts = sections + 1**

Then spread the sections evenly along the run rather than ending with one short section. Corner posts are shared by two runs, so count them only once. Each gate needs a post on both sides, and gate posts take more load than line posts — many builders use a larger post (such as a 6×6) or set them deeper, with more concrete.

For vinyl and many metal systems, post spacing is fixed by the panel width, so use the manufacturer's spacing instead.

## Step 3: Rails

Rails (usually 2×4s) span between posts and carry the pickets.

| Fence height | Typical rails per section |
|---|---|
| Up to about 4 ft | 2 |
| 5–6 ft | 3 |
| 8 ft | 3–4 |

A middle rail on taller fences stops pickets from bowing. Rail count = sections × rails per section.

![Elevation of one 8 foot fence section 6 feet tall, with posts set 30 inches into concrete on 6 inches of gravel, three 2 by 4 rails and 1 by 6 pickets with small gaps](/images/blog/how-to-plan-a-fence/fence-section.svg)

## Step 4: Pickets

Pickets are counted by the length of fence they cover. Remember that a "1×6" picket is actually about 5.5 in wide.

> **Pickets = fence length (in) ÷ (picket width + gap)**

| Style | Gap | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Privacy (side by side) | 0 to about 1/4 in | Boards shrink as they dry, so tight-fitting wet boards open up |
| Board-on-board | Overlap of about 1 in or more | Two layers; with 5.5 in boards overlapping 1–1.5 in, roughly 1.2–1.4 times the boards of a single tight layer |
| Picket (spaced) | 2–3 in | Classic front-yard style; check pool and pet gap requirements |

Add **about 10%** for split, warped or badly knotted boards and for cuts at gates and ends. The [material waste factor guide](/blog/material-waste-factor-guide) explains why lumber needs this allowance.

## Step 5: Post length and hole size

**Hole depth:** A common rule is one-third to one-half of the above-ground height, and below the frost line in cold climates. For a 6-ft fence, 2.5–3 ft is typical. Your local code may set a minimum.

**Hole diameter:** About three times the post width — 10–12 in for a 4×4 (3.5 in actual) and 12–14 in for a 6×6.

**Post length:** above-ground height + depth in concrete + a few inches if you will trim the tops level. A 6-ft fence with posts set 30 in deep needs 8.5 ft of post, so you would buy 10-ft posts and trim them, or set 8-ft posts 2 ft deep if your soil and code allow.

Use lumber rated for **ground contact** for posts. Many builders put a few inches of gravel in the bottom of each hole for drainage and crown the concrete above grade so water runs away from the post.

## Step 6: Concrete

For each post, the concrete volume is the hole volume minus the post volume. A 4×4 post in a 10-in hole with 30 in of concrete needs about 1.15 cu ft — roughly **two 80 lb bags**. Our guide on [how many bags of concrete](/blog/how-many-bags-of-concrete) shows the full calculation, and the [post hole calculator](/calculators/post-hole-calculator) handles other sizes.

## Step 7: Gates and hardware

- Leave the gate opening equal to the gate width plus the clearances specified by your hinge and latch hardware.
- Build or buy a gate with a diagonal brace running from the bottom hinge side up to the top latch side, so it resists sagging. Anti-sag cable kits are an alternative.
- Use heavy-duty hinges rated for the gate's weight, and a latch suited to its use (self-closing for pools).

## Worked example: a 100-ft L-shaped privacy fence

- **Run A:** 60 ft
- **Run B:** 40 ft, including a 4-ft walk gate at the far end
- **Height:** 6 ft, wood privacy with 1×6 pickets and 1/4-in gaps
- **Post spacing:** 8 ft maximum, 4×4 posts in 10-in holes, 30 in of concrete on 6 in of gravel

**Posts**

- Run A: 60 ÷ 8 = 7.5 → 8 sections at 7.5 ft → **9 posts** (including the corner)
- Run B: 40 − 4 ft gate = 36 ft of fence. 36 ÷ 8 = 4.5 → 5 sections at 7.2 ft → 6 posts, minus the shared corner = **5 posts**
- Second gate post on the far side of the opening: **1 post**
- **Total: 15 posts**, 10 ft long (trimmed). Consider 6×6 posts for the two gate posts.

**Rails:** 13 sections × 3 = **39 rails** (2×4 × 8 ft), plus lumber for the gate frame.

**Pickets:**

- Fence length: 60 + 36 = 96 ft = 1,152 in
- 1,152 ÷ (5.5 + 0.25) = 200.3 → 201 pickets
- Plus 10%: **221 pickets**
- Gate: 48 in ÷ 5.75 = 8.3 → **9 pickets**
- **Total: about 230 pickets**

**Concrete:** 15 posts × 2 bags = 30 bags of 80 lb, plus a couple more for larger gate-post holes: **about 32 bags**.

**Fasteners:** 2 per picket per rail: 230 × 3 × 2 = 1,380. Buy **about 1,500** exterior-rated screws or ring-shank nails (hot-dip galvanised or stainless steel, compatible with treated lumber). Add structural screws or brackets for rail-to-post connections.

**Gravel:** a few inches in each hole; one or two bags per hole, or a small bulk order.

## Finishing and maintenance

New pressure-treated wood often needs time to dry before it will accept stain — follow the stain manufacturer's guidance on timing. Staining both sides of a privacy fence is a bigger job than people expect; the [fence stain calculator](/calculators/fence-stain-calculator) estimates gallons from length and height.

## What drives the cost

Material prices vary widely by region, species and timing, so build your own list using local prices. The main cost drivers are fence length, height, material (pressure-treated pine, cedar, vinyl, aluminium, chain link), the number of gates, terrain (slopes need stepped or racked sections), rocky soil, removal of an old fence, and labour rates. ## Safety notes

- Call 811 (US) before digging and wait for marks.
- Wear eye and hearing protection when cutting; use a dust mask when cutting treated lumber.
- Power augers can kick hard when they hit roots or rocks; use two-person augers with care or hire help.
- Wet concrete is caustic — wear gloves and eye protection.
