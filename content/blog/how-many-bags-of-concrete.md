Concrete is sold two ways: in bags of dry premix you mix with water, or as ready-mix delivered by truck. For a few fence posts or a small pad, bags are perfect. For a driveway, nobody sensible mixes 200 bags by hand. The awkward zone is in between — patios, shed pads and long fence runs — and that is where knowing the numbers saves money and your back.

This guide shows how to calculate bags for any job, gives worked examples for a slab and for fence posts, and explains how to decide between bags and ready-mix. The [concrete bags calculator](/calculators/concrete-bags-calculator) does the arithmetic instantly.

## How much concrete does a bag make?

Bags are sold by weight, but you need volume. Standard premix takes roughly 133 lb of dry mix per cubic foot of placed concrete, which gives these common yields:

| Bag size | Approximate yield | Bags per cubic yard |
|---|---|---|
| 40 lb | 0.30 cu ft | 90 |
| 50 lb | 0.375 cu ft | 72 |
| 60 lb | 0.45 cu ft | 60 |
| 80 lb | 0.60 cu ft | 45 |

Yields vary a little between products — fast-setting post mixes, high-strength mixes and fibre-reinforced mixes can differ — so check the yield printed on the bag you are buying.

## The formula

1. **Find the volume in cubic feet:** length × width × thickness, all in feet. Convert inches to feet by dividing by 12 (4 in = 0.333 ft).
2. **Add a waste allowance:** 5–10% for spillage, uneven subgrade and form flex. Use 10% if the ground is not perfectly level.
3. **Divide by the bag yield** and round up.

> **Bags = length × width × (thickness in ÷ 12) × 1.10 ÷ bag yield**

If you need the volume in cubic yards, divide cubic feet by 27 — our guide to [cubic yards](/blog/cubic-yards-explained) explains the conversion in detail.

## Worked example 1: an 8 × 10 ft shed pad

![Cross-section of a 4 inch concrete slab on 4 inches of compacted gravel base, with wire mesh or rebar placed in the middle of the slab and form boards at the edges](/images/blog/how-many-bags-of-concrete/slab-section.svg)

- Size: 8 × 10 ft, 4 in thick
- Volume: 8 × 10 × 0.333 = **26.7 cu ft** (0.99 cu yd)
- With 10% waste: 26.7 × 1.10 = **29.3 cu ft**
- 80 lb bags: 29.3 ÷ 0.6 = 48.9 → **49 bags**
- 60 lb bags: 29.3 ÷ 0.45 = 65.1 → **66 bags**

Forty-nine 80 lb bags is nearly **4,000 lb** of material. That is more than many pickups can safely carry in one load, and it is a lot to lift, pour and mix. Most people would rent a drum mixer for this size — or seriously consider ready-mix.

Four inches is a common thickness for patios, walkways and shed pads. Driveways and slabs that will carry vehicles are typically thicker and reinforced; check local requirements. The [concrete slab calculator](/calculators/concrete-slab-calculator) lets you try different thicknesses.

## Worked example 2: setting fence posts

Post holes are cylinders with a post in the middle, so you calculate the hole volume and subtract the post.

![Cross-section of a fence post hole 10 inches in diameter and 36 inches deep, with 6 inches of gravel at the bottom, 30 inches of concrete around a 4 by 4 post, and the concrete crowned above grade to shed water](/images/blog/how-many-bags-of-concrete/post-hole.svg)

- Hole: 10 in diameter, 36 in deep
- 6 in of gravel at the bottom for drainage, so 30 in of concrete
- Post: 4 × 4 (actual size 3.5 × 3.5 in)

**Hole volume:** 3.1416 × 5² × 30 = **2,356 cu in**

**Post volume:** 3.5 × 3.5 × 30 = **368 cu in**

**Concrete:** 2,356 − 368 = 1,988 cu in ÷ 1,728 = **1.15 cu ft per post**

That is 1.15 ÷ 0.6 = 1.9 → **2 bags of 80 lb per post**. A 12-post fence needs about **24 bags**. Fast-setting post mixes often have a different yield per bag, so recalculate with the figure on the bag. The [post hole calculator](/calculators/post-hole-calculator) handles different hole sizes and post shapes, and our guide on [how to plan a fence](/blog/how-to-plan-a-fence) covers post spacing and depth.

### Hole size rules of thumb

- **Diameter:** about three times the post width — roughly 10–12 in for a 4×4 and 12–14 in for a 6×6.
- **Depth:** commonly one-third to one-half of the above-ground post height, and below your local frost line where the ground freezes.

## Bags vs ready-mix: how to decide

| Factor | Bagged premix | Ready-mix delivery |
|---|---|---|
| Best for | Up to about 1 cu yd; posts, footings, small pads, repairs | About 1 cu yd and up; patios, driveways, foundations |
| Cost structure | Per bag, plus mixer rental if needed | Per cubic yard, plus delivery; small loads often have a short-load fee |
| Labour | High — lifting, mixing and pouring every bag | Low — the truck delivers mixed concrete |
| Consistency | Varies batch to batch if mixed by hand | Uniform mix from the plant |
| Timing | Work at your own pace (but avoid cold joints) | Must be ready when the truck arrives; time on site is limited |
| Access | Anywhere you can carry a bag | Truck needs access, or you pay for a pump or wheelbarrows |

There is no universal break-even point, because bag and delivery prices vary widely by region and over time. Many DIYers find that **around 1 cubic yard (about 45 bags of 80 lb)** is where ready-mix starts to make sense — not only on cost but because mixing 45+ bags fast enough to place one continuous slab is genuinely hard work.

Between the two, some areas offer **trailer loads** of mixed concrete you tow home, or **volumetric (mobile-mix) trucks** that mix on site so you pay only for what you use. Both are worth a phone call for jobs of 1–3 yards.

To compare costs for your area, get a quote per cubic yard including delivery and any short-load fee, price the bags and mixer rental locally, and plug both into the [concrete cost calculator](/calculators/concrete-cost-calculator).

### A quick comparison for the 8 × 10 pad

| | Bags | Ready-mix |
|---|---|---|
| Quantity | 49 × 80 lb | 1 cu yd (or 1.25 for safety) |
| Extra costs | Mixer rental, delivery of bags or several trips | Delivery and likely short-load fee |
| People needed | 2–3 for a steady pour | 2 to spread and finish |
| Time | Several hours of mixing | Under an hour to place |

In this case the material costs may be close, and the deciding factor is often labour and access.

## Choosing the right mix

- **General-purpose premix:** Slabs, steps, footings and posts.
- **High-strength mix:** Thinner sections and areas with heavier loads; follow the manufacturer's guidance.
- **Fast-setting post mix:** Poured dry into the hole and soaked with water; sets in under an hour, convenient for fences but not designed for slabs.
- **Fibre-reinforced or crack-resistant mix:** Helps control shrinkage cracking in thin sections.

Concrete needs to cure, not just dry. Keep it damp or covered for several days, particularly in hot or windy weather, and keep heavy loads off until the manufacturer says it is ready.

## Safety notes

- **Wet concrete is caustic.** It can cause serious skin burns. Wear waterproof gloves, long sleeves, long trousers and eye protection, and wash skin promptly.
- **Dry mix contains silica dust.** Open and mix bags in a ventilated area and wear a dust mask.
- **Lift with care.** An 80 lb bag is heavy; a wheelbarrow or hand truck helps.
- **Call 811** (in the US) a few days before digging post holes or footings, so buried utilities can be marked.
- **Structural work** such as foundations, load-bearing footings and retaining walls may need permits, inspections or engineering. Check with your local building department, and use a qualified professional when in doubt. The [footing calculator](/calculators/footing-calculator) estimates volume, not structural adequacy.

## Quick reference

- 80 lb bag ≈ 0.6 cu ft; 45 bags per cubic yard
- 60 lb bag ≈ 0.45 cu ft; 60 bags per cubic yard
- Slab: length × width × thickness (ft), plus 10%, divided by bag yield
- Post: hole volume minus post volume
- Consider ready-mix from about 1 cubic yard
