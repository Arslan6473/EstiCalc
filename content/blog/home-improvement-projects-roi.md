"Will this add value to my house?" is one of the most common questions behind any renovation decision. The honest answer is that most projects add *some* resale value, few add as much as they cost, and the ones that make the most sense depend on your house, your market and how long you'll stay.

Rather than repeat national averages that may not apply to your home, this guide explains how to think about return on investment (ROI): what to measure, which kinds of value count, and how to run the numbers for your own project. The [Home Improvement ROI Calculator](/calculators/home-improvement-roi-calculator) does the arithmetic once you have estimates.

## What "ROI" means for a house

In remodeling, ROI is usually expressed as **cost recovered**: the share of the project cost that shows up as higher home value when you sell.

**Cost recovered (%) = increase in home value ÷ project cost × 100**

If a $20,000 project raises your home's sale price by $14,000, you recovered 70% of the cost. That's a "loss" of $6,000 on paper, but you also had the use of the improvement for however many years you lived with it.

This is different from the investment-world idea of ROI, where you'd expect to get back more than you put in. With homes, recovering 100% or more is the exception.

## Three kinds of return

A realistic view of ROI counts all three.

![Diagram of a project's cost on one side of a balance and three kinds of return on the other: resale value, money saved, and use and enjoyment](/images/blog/home-improvement-projects-roi/diagram-1.svg)

### 1. Resale value

The extra amount a buyer will pay because of the improvement. It depends on:

- **Neighborhood ceiling.** A buyer will only pay so much for a house on a given street. Improvements that push a home well above comparable homes nearby usually recover less.
- **Buyer expectations.** In some markets, buyers expect central air or a second bathroom; lacking them hurts more than having them helps.
- **Condition and timing.** Improvements age. A kitchen remodeled 15 years before sale won't carry the same premium as a new one.
- **Taste.** Highly personal choices (bold colors, unusual layouts) can reduce appeal.

### 2. Money saved

Some projects pay you back during ownership:

- **Energy upgrades** such as attic insulation, air sealing and efficient heating and cooling lower utility bills. See the [attic insulation guide](/blog/attic-insulation-guide) and [how to size an air conditioner](/blog/how-to-size-an-air-conditioner).
- **Maintenance and repair** projects avoid bigger costs later. A roof replaced before it leaks avoids water damage to ceilings, insulation and framing.
- **Durable materials** reduce replacement and upkeep, for example choosing lower-maintenance decking or flooring.

### 3. Use and enjoyment

A bigger kitchen for a family that cooks, a deck you use every summer evening, or an accessible bathroom that lets you stay in your home longer have value that no appraisal records. This is often the main reason to renovate, and it's legitimate to weigh it.

## Categories of projects and how they tend to behave

Without inventing percentages, it's possible to describe the general patterns:

| Project type | Examples | How the return usually shows up |
|---|---|---|
| Necessary repairs | Roof, leaks, foundation, failing systems | Avoids price cuts and inspection problems; buyers expect it done |
| Curb appeal and exterior | Entry door, garage door, paint, landscaping | Affects first impressions and buyer interest |
| Energy efficiency | Insulation, air sealing, windows, HVAC | Savings during ownership; sometimes rebates or tax credits |
| Minor cosmetic | Interior paint, fixtures, hardware, flooring refresh | Low cost relative to visual impact |
| Major remodels | Kitchens, bathrooms, additions | Large expense; partial recovery; big lifestyle benefit |
| Highly personal | Pools, specialty rooms, luxury finishes | Value depends heavily on local buyer preferences |

Industry surveys such as the annual Cost vs. Value report published by *Remodeling* magazine compare the estimated cost and resale value of common projects across US regions. They're useful for seeing patterns, but the figures are averages based on estimates, not results from actual sales of your house. A local real estate agent and recent comparable sales are better guides.

## How to estimate ROI for your project

### Step 1: Get a realistic cost

Use detailed estimates, including permits, disposal and a contingency. The [Renovation Budget Calculator](/calculators/renovation-budget-calculator) helps build one. For major rooms, see the [kitchen remodel budget guide](/blog/kitchen-remodel-budget-guide) and [bathroom remodel cost guide](/blog/bathroom-remodel-cost-guide).

### Step 2: Estimate the value increase

Ask a local agent or appraiser two questions: what would the house sell for today as-is, and what would it sell for with the project done? Compare recently sold homes that are similar except for the feature in question.

### Step 3: Estimate savings during ownership

For energy projects, estimate annual savings from your current bills and the project's expected improvement, then multiply by the years you'll stay. Keep it conservative; actual savings depend on weather, usage and energy prices.

### Step 4: Decide how long you'll stay

The longer you stay, the more weight savings and enjoyment carry, and the less a resale premium matters (it also shrinks as the improvement ages).

### Step 5: Combine

**Total return = value increase at sale + cumulative savings**
**Return ratio = total return ÷ project cost**

## Worked example

You're weighing two projects, each about $12,000, and expect to stay 7 years.

**Project A: Attic insulation and air sealing plus a new heat pump water heater.**
- Agent's view: little direct resale premium, maybe **$2,000**, mostly as a selling point.
- Your estimated savings: **$700 per year** (based on your bills; illustrative).
- Over 7 years: $700 × 7 = **$4,900**.
- Any rebate or credit you qualify for: say **$1,500** (check current programs).
- Total return: $2,000 + $4,900 + $1,500 = **$8,400**, or **70%** of cost.

**Project B: Cosmetic kitchen refresh (paint cabinets, new counters and backsplash).**
- Agent's view: the dated kitchen is the main buyer objection; with updates the home might sell for **$9,000** more.
- Savings: none.
- Total return: **$9,000**, or **75%** of cost.

On paper the two are close. The deciding factors become which one you'd enjoy more, how sure you are about the agent's estimate, and how long you'll really stay. If you might sell in two years, Project B probably makes more sense; if you'll stay ten years, Project A's savings keep adding up. These figures are illustrations, so plug in your own.

## Common ROI mistakes

- **Overbuilding for the neighborhood.** Premium finishes in a modest neighborhood rarely pay back.
- **Ignoring deferred maintenance.** A new kitchen won't offset a buyer's concern about an old roof or wet basement.
- **Forgetting financing costs.** Interest on a loan for the project reduces the return.
- **Assuming national averages apply.** Local markets vary widely.
- **Skipping permits.** Unpermitted work can create problems at sale and may need to be redone.
- **Counting only resale.** Savings and enjoyment are real returns.

## Safety and quality

Whatever you choose, do it properly. Structural, electrical, gas and roofing work should meet code, be permitted where required and be done by qualified people. Poor-quality or unsafe work can reduce value rather than add it, and an inspection during a sale is likely to find it.

## Quick checklist

- ROI in remodeling usually means percentage of cost recovered at sale.
- Count resale value, savings and enjoyment.
- Fix necessary repairs before discretionary upgrades.
- Don't overbuild for your neighborhood.
- Use local agents and comparable sales, not national averages alone.
- Factor in how long you'll stay.
