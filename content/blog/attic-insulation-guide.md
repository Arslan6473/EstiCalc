If your upstairs is hot in summer, cold in winter, or your energy bills feel high, the attic is usually the first place to look. Heat moves out of a home through the top in winter and the hot roof radiates down in summer. Adding insulation on the attic floor is one of the most commonly recommended energy upgrades because the space is often accessible and the work is straightforward.

This guide explains R-value, the recommended levels by climate zone, how batts and blown-in insulation compare, and how to estimate how much you need. When you have your attic's measurements, the [Attic Insulation Calculator](/calculators/attic-insulation-calculator) converts them into bags or rolls.

## What R-value means

R-value measures resistance to heat flow. The higher the number, the better the material insulates. R-values add up in layers: if you have R19 already and add R30 on top, you end up with roughly R49.

Two points matter more than anything else:

- **R-value depends on thickness.** Each material has an R-value per inch. More inches means more R.
- **Installed R-value depends on installation.** Compressed batts, gaps around joists and settled loose-fill all deliver less than the label suggests.

## Recommended attic R-values by climate zone

The US is divided into climate zones from 1 (hottest, such as south Florida and Hawaii) to 8 (coldest, parts of Alaska). ENERGY STAR publishes recommended insulation levels for each zone, based on the model energy code. For attics, the recommendations look like this:

| Climate zone | Uninsulated attic | Attic with 3–4 in existing |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | R30 to R49 | R25 to R30 |
| 2 | R49 to R60 | R38 to R49 |
| 3 | R49 to R60 | R38 to R49 |
| 4 | R60 | R38 to R49 |
| 5–8 | R60 | R49 |

These are the levels ENERGY STAR lists on its recommended home insulation R-values page at the time of writing. Recommendations are updated as energy codes change, so check energystar.gov for the current table and confirm your county's climate zone on the map provided there or by the Department of Energy. Your local building department may also require a specific minimum when insulation is part of permitted work.

## Batts vs blown-in insulation

### Batts and rolls

Fiberglass and mineral wool batts are pre-cut blankets sized to fit between joists spaced 16 or 24 inches on center.

- **Pros:** No machine needed, easy to handle in open attics, predictable thickness.
- **Cons:** Harder to fit snugly around wiring, blocking and irregular framing; gaps and compression reduce performance; reaching very high R-values takes multiple layers.
- **Tip:** When adding a second layer, run it perpendicular to the joists to cover the tops of the framing, which otherwise act as thermal bridges.

### Blown-in (loose-fill)

Loose-fill cellulose or fiberglass is blown through a hose with a machine (many home centers lend or rent one with a bag purchase).

- **Pros:** Fills around obstructions, covers joists completely, fast to bring up to R49–R60, works well over existing insulation.
- **Cons:** Needs a machine and a helper; messy; depth must be checked with rulers; can be blown into soffit vents if baffles are missing.

### Typical R-value per inch

The Department of Energy lists these approximate ranges:

| Material | Approx. R-value per inch |
|---|---|
| Fiberglass batts | R-2.9 to R-3.8 |
| Mineral wool batts | R-3.0 to R-3.3 |
| Loose-fill cellulose | R-3.2 to R-3.8 |
| Loose-fill fiberglass | R-2.2 to R-2.7 |

Use the product's own label for estimating. Loose-fill bags include a coverage chart listing, for each R-value, the minimum settled thickness, the installed thickness and how many bags cover 1,000 square feet.

## How much insulation do you need?

### Step 1: Measure the attic floor area

Measure the length and width of the insulated attic floor, usually matching the footprint of the heated rooms below. Don't include garage ceilings or porches unless you intend to insulate them. For L-shaped footprints, split into rectangles; see [how to calculate square footage](/blog/how-to-calculate-square-footage). Subtract large areas you will not insulate, such as a floored storage platform.

### Step 2: Measure what's already there

Push a ruler down through the insulation to the drywall in several spots and note the average depth and type. Multiply depth by the per-inch R-value to estimate your existing level.

### Step 3: Find the R-value to add

Target R-value minus existing R-value equals the R-value to add.

### Step 4: Convert to bags or rolls

- **Loose-fill:** Read the bag chart for the target R-value and divide your area by the coverage per bag.
- **Batts:** Each package lists the square footage it covers at a specific R-value. Divide your area by that number and round up.

### Worked example

A 1,200 square foot attic in climate zone 5 has about 4 inches of old fiberglass batts.

- Existing: 4 in × about R-3.1 ≈ **R12**
- Target from the table: **R60** (the "3–4 in existing" column suggests adding R49)
- To add: roughly **R48–R49**

Suppose a cellulose bag chart says R49 needs 16 bags per 1,000 square feet (this is an illustrative figure; use your product's chart). Bags needed: 1,200 ÷ 1,000 × 16 = 19.2, so buy **20 bags**, plus one or two spares for low spots.

If you chose batts instead, an R30 package covering, say, 50 square feet and an R19 package covering 75 square feet would require 24 and 16 packages respectively. Again, the coverage figures are examples, so check the label.

## Prep work that matters as much as R-value

Insulation slows heat moving through materials, but air leaks carry heat around it. Before adding insulation:

1. **Air seal.** Seal gaps around plumbing vents, wiring holes, duct chases, light fixtures and top plates with caulk or canned foam. Use fire-rated sealant around flues and chimneys.
2. **Install baffles** at the eaves so insulation does not block soffit ventilation.
3. **Keep clearances.** Maintain manufacturer- and code-required clearances around chimneys, flues and recessed lights not rated for insulation contact (IC-rated). Build dams where needed.
4. **Weatherstrip and insulate the attic hatch.** An uninsulated hatch is a big hole in an otherwise well-insulated ceiling.
5. **Mark depth rulers** on joists across the attic before blowing so you can see when you've reached the target.
6. **Fix moisture problems first.** Roof leaks, bathroom fans venting into the attic and mold should be resolved before covering anything.

## Vapor retarders

In cold climates, a vapor retarder usually goes on the warm-in-winter side of the insulation, which for an attic floor is the ceiling below. When adding a layer over existing insulation, use unfaced batts or loose-fill so you don't create a second vapor retarder that could trap moisture. Requirements vary by climate zone, so check your local code.

## Safety note

Attics are hazardous work areas. Step only on joists or a walk board; stepping between joists will put your foot through the ceiling. Wear a properly fitted respirator rated for fine particles, eye protection, gloves and long sleeves. Watch for exposed nails in the roof deck, and avoid working in the attic during the heat of the day. Do not cover or disturb old knob-and-tube wiring, and if you find loose, gray-brown or shiny vermiculite insulation, the EPA advises not to disturb it because it may contain asbestos; have it assessed by a professional.
