A kitchen remodel is usually the most expensive room project in a house, and it's also the one most prone to budget creep. A countertop upgrade here, a relocated sink there, and a mid-range plan can quietly become a high-end one. The fix is not a magic number from the internet; it's a budget you build line by line, with a contingency you don't touch unless you have to.

This guide shows how to set a total, divide it among the major categories, and protect it with a contingency. Use the [Kitchen Remodel Cost Calculator](/calculators/kitchen-remodel-cost-calculator) for a quick starting estimate and the [Renovation Budget Calculator](/calculators/renovation-budget-calculator) to track your line items.

## Start with scope, not finishes

Cost is driven mostly by **scope**: how much you change. Finishes matter, but the biggest jumps come from moving things.

| Scope level | What it typically includes | Rough US range* |
|---|---|---|
| Cosmetic refresh | Paint, cabinet refacing or painting, new hardware, faucet, lighting, backsplash, maybe counters | Low tens of thousands or less |
| Mid-range remodel | New stock or semi-custom cabinets, new counters, new appliances, flooring, same layout | Mid tens of thousands |
| Major remodel | Custom cabinets, premium counters and appliances, layout changes, moved plumbing or walls | High tens of thousands to six figures |

*Ranges are broad and vary heavily by region, kitchen size, home age and date. Use them only to sanity-check quotes.

Before collecting quotes, write down:

- Will the sink, range and refrigerator stay where they are?
- Are any walls coming out? Are any of them load-bearing?
- Are you replacing the flooring, and does it run under the cabinets?
- Which appliances are staying?
- Do you need new circuits for an induction range, microwave or additional outlets?

Each "yes" to a move or a new circuit adds trades, permits and time.

## Split the budget into categories

There is no fixed formula, but a starting allocation like the one below helps you notice early if one category is crowding out the others. Adjust it to your priorities.

![Bar chart showing a 50,000 dollar kitchen budget split into cabinets, labor, appliances, countertops, flooring, electrical and lighting, plumbing, finishes, and a separate 15 percent contingency](/images/blog/kitchen-remodel-budget-guide/diagram-1.svg)

| Category | Rough share of project budget |
|---|---|
| Cabinets and hardware | 25–35% |
| Installation and general labor | 15–25% |
| Appliances | 10–20% |
| Countertops | 8–15% |
| Flooring | 4–8% |
| Electrical and lighting | 4–8% |
| Plumbing fixtures and labor | 3–8% |
| Walls, ceiling, paint, backsplash | 3–7% |
| Design, permits, disposal | 2–5% |

These are planning ranges based on how kitchen costs are typically structured, not data from a study. Your quotes will tell you the real split.

## Worked example: a $50,000 budget

Suppose you have $50,000 available in total. First, set aside the contingency. For a 1970s house where you'll replace the floor and add circuits, 15% is sensible:

- Contingency: $50,000 × 0.15 = **$7,500**
- Working budget: $50,000 − $7,500 = **$42,500**

Now allocate the working budget:

| Category | Share | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Cabinets and hardware | 30% | $12,750 |
| Labor and installation | 20% | $8,500 |
| Appliances | 14% | $5,950 |
| Countertops | 12% | $5,100 |
| Flooring | 6% | $2,550 |
| Electrical and lighting | 6% | $2,550 |
| Plumbing | 5% | $2,125 |
| Walls, paint, backsplash | 4% | $1,700 |
| Permits, design, disposal | 3% | $1,275 |
| **Total** | **100%** | **$42,500** |

If your quotes come back and the counters you love cost $8,000, you can see immediately that $2,900 must come from somewhere else, perhaps by keeping the existing refrigerator or choosing a simpler backsplash, not from the contingency.

## Get material quantities right

Accurate quantities keep material lines honest:

- **Flooring:** measure the room and decide whether flooring runs under the cabinets. See [how to measure a room for flooring](/blog/how-to-measure-a-room-for-flooring) and compare options in [laminate vs vinyl plank flooring](/blog/laminate-vs-vinyl-plank-flooring).
- **Backsplash:** measure each wall section; our [backsplash tile guide](/blog/backsplash-tile-guide) walks through it.
- **Paint:** walls and ceiling usually need only a gallon or two in a kitchen; see [how much paint do I need](/blog/how-much-paint-do-i-need).
- **Drywall:** if walls are opened for wiring or plumbing, budget for patches using the [drywall estimating guide](/blog/drywall-estimating-guide).

Always include a waste allowance on cut materials; our [material waste factor guide](/blog/material-waste-factor-guide) explains typical percentages.

## Understand how contractors price the job

A general contractor's bid includes subcontractor costs, materials, overhead and profit. Overhead and profit are usually applied as a markup on costs. Understanding the difference between markup and margin helps you read a bid without assuming you're being overcharged; see [contractor markup vs margin](/blog/contractor-markup-vs-margin) and the [Contractor Markup Calculator](/calculators/contractor-markup-calculator).

When comparing bids:

- Ask for line items, not a single lump sum.
- Check what allowances are included (for example, "$3,000 allowance for tile"). Low allowances make a bid look cheaper than it will be.
- Confirm who pulls permits, who handles disposal, and whether cleanup is included.
- Ask for the payment schedule and tie payments to completed milestones.

## Hidden costs to plan for

These items regularly surprise kitchen remodelers:

- **Electrical upgrades.** Modern kitchens often need several dedicated circuits, and older panels may lack capacity.
- **Plumbing surprises.** Corroded supply lines or drains found behind walls.
- **Subfloor repair** under an old sink or dishwasher.
- **Venting a range hood** to the exterior.
- **Living without a kitchen.** Extra takeout and eating out for several weeks.
- **Delivery and haul-away** fees for appliances.
- **Trim, filler strips and panels** for cabinets.

The contingency exists for these. Keep it separate in your tracking so it's visible when it gets spent.

## Ways to save without regrets

- **Keep the layout.** Leaving the sink, range and dishwasher where they are avoids most plumbing and gas work.
- **Mix cabinet levels.** Use stock or semi-custom boxes with upgraded doors or hardware.
- **Reface or paint** solid cabinets instead of replacing them.
- **Splurge selectively.** Put premium material on the island top and a durable, cheaper surface on the perimeter.
- **Do the low-risk tasks yourself:** demolition of cabinets (not walls), painting, backsplash tile, hardware.
- **Order early.** Rush shipping and change orders are expensive.

## Safety and permits

Kitchen remodels commonly involve electrical, plumbing and sometimes gas or structural work. These usually require permits and licensed trades. Never remove a wall until a qualified person has confirmed whether it is load-bearing, and never disconnect or move a gas line yourself. Permits protect you: inspections catch problems before the walls are closed, and unpermitted work can complicate a future sale.

## Does a kitchen remodel pay back?

Kitchen updates can make a home more appealing to buyers, but few remodels return their full cost at sale. How much you recover depends on your market, the home's value, and whether the remodel matches the neighborhood. ## Quick checklist

- Define scope first: what moves and what stays.
- Set aside a 10–20% contingency before allocating.
- Allocate the rest across categories and track actual quotes against it.
- Measure materials carefully and include waste.
- Compare bids line by line, including allowances.
- Pull permits and use licensed trades for electrical, plumbing and gas.
