No matter how carefully you measure, the amount of material you buy is never exactly the area you calculate. Boards are cut to length, tiles are cut around corners, some pieces arrive damaged, and loose materials settle. Professionals plan for this with a **waste factor** (also called overage or waste allowance): an extra percentage added to the measured quantity.

Get it too low and you run short mid-project. Get it too high and you pay for material you don't use. This guide lists typical waste factors by material, explains what pushes them up or down, and shows how to apply them correctly. The [waste percentage calculator](/calculators/waste-percentage-calculator) applies any percentage for you.

## Where waste comes from

- **Cut-offs:** The end of every row of flooring, the edges of tiled floors, drywall around doors.
- **Pattern and layout:** Diagonal and herringbone layouts, wallpaper pattern repeats, stripes in carpet.
- **Breakage and defects:** Chipped tiles, split boards, warped studs, damaged sheet corners.
- **Mistakes:** Mis-measured or mis-cut pieces happen to everyone.
- **Settling and compaction:** Gravel, soil and mulch occupy less volume once spread and compacted.
- **Spillage and residue:** Concrete left in the mixer, paint in the tray and roller.
- **Spares:** A few extra pieces kept for future repairs — often the most valuable "waste" of all.

## Typical waste factors by material

These are common planning ranges for typical residential projects. The right figure for your job depends on the complexity of the space, the material and your experience.

## What pushes the waste factor up or down

**Use a higher allowance when:**

- The room or area has many corners, angles, columns or obstacles.
- The layout is diagonal, herringbone, or has a pattern to match.
- The material is large relative to the space (big tiles in a small bathroom).
- The material is fragile, natural, or varies in colour (stone, handmade tile, hardwood).
- You are new to the task.
- The product is special-order, discontinued soon, or hard to match later.

**You can use a lower allowance when:**

- The space is a simple rectangle.
- The material is small and offcuts are reusable (starting the next row of flooring with the previous row's off-cut).
- You can easily buy more of the exact same product, or return extras.
- The rounding up to whole units already provides a healthy margin.

## How to apply a waste factor

The standard method is to **multiply**:

> **Quantity to buy = measured quantity × (1 + waste %)**

For 200 sq ft of flooring at 10%: 200 × 1.10 = **220 sq ft**.

Some estimators divide instead — 200 ÷ (1 − 0.10) = 222 sq ft — on the logic that 10% of what you *buy* will be wasted. The difference is small at normal percentages, and either approach is fine as long as you are consistent. At high percentages (20% and up), dividing gives a noticeably larger number.

### Apply waste before rounding

Add the waste factor to the measured quantity first, then round up to the units you can buy. Rounding first and then adding waste double-counts your margin.

## Worked example: a hallway and bedroom in vinyl plank

- Bedroom: 12 × 13 ft = 156 sq ft, simple rectangle → 7%
- Hallway: 3.5 × 18 ft = 63 sq ft, three doorways and a closet → 15%

**With waste:**

- Bedroom: 156 × 1.07 = 166.9 sq ft
- Hallway: 63 × 1.15 = 72.5 sq ft
- Total: **239.4 sq ft**

**Round to boxes:** at 23.6 sq ft per box, 239.4 ÷ 23.6 = 10.1 → **11 boxes** (259.6 sq ft).

Notice that rounding up added another 20 sq ft. Ten boxes (236 sq ft) would give only about 7.8% extra overall — less than this hallway needs — so 11 boxes is the right answer. If you had applied a flat 15% to everything, you would have needed 219 × 1.15 = 251.9 sq ft, which is still 11 boxes, so here the rounding absorbs the difference. With a different box size, it might not.

## Buying too much vs too little

| | Buying too little | Buying too much |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Extra trips, delivery fees, delays | Paying for unused material |
| Matching | New batch may differ in colour or size | Spare stock from the same batch |
| Schedule | Installers or concrete may be waiting | No impact |
| Recovery | Sometimes impossible if discontinued | Return unopened units (check policy) |

For finishes that must match — tile, flooring, wallpaper, paint colours, brick — leaning slightly over is usually the safer choice. Keep at least one unopened box or a handful of spare pieces, labelled with the product name, colour and lot number, for future repairs.

For loose bulk materials like gravel and concrete, the cost of running short mid-pour is high, so most people order a little extra and find a use for leftovers.

## Quick reference

- Paint: 10%
- Plank flooring: 5–10% straight, 15% diagonal, 15–20% herringbone
- Tile: 10% straight, 15% diagonal, 15–20% complex or large format
- Drywall and lumber: 10–15%
- Roofing shingles: about 10% gable, 15% hip
- Concrete and mulch: 5–10%
- Multiply by (1 + waste %), then round up to whole units
